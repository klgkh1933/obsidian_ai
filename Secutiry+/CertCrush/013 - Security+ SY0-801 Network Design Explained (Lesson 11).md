## 01:18 — Security zones, VLANs and segmentation ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X_nHCg144zE&t=78s))

Segmentation means splitting your network into separate zones, so traffic between them can be controlled and the breach in one zone doesn't spill into the others. Each zone is a neighborhood with its own level of trust and its own rules. The everyday version is a submarine. Ships are built with watertight compartments, so if one section floods, sealed doors keep the water from sinking the whole vessel. Zones are the compartments and the checkpoints between them are the sealed doors. Most designs share a few familiar zones. The untrusted internet, the screen subnet for public-f facing servers, the trusted internal zone, restricted zones for databases and payment systems, a guest zone, and a management zone where only administrators work.

The usual way to build zones is with VLANs or virtual local area networks. A VLAN carves one physical switch into several logical networks that behave like separate switches. Your finance department and your guest Wi-Fi can share the same hardware while living in completely separate VLANs. Why bother? Because on a flat network, one infected laptop can attack the finance servers without crossing a single checkpoint. Zone shrink the blast radius of any breach.

## 16:30 — Network attacks from DDoS to DNS ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X_nHCg144zE&t=990s))

take them one at a time. First, DDoS. A denial of service attack tries to make a service unavailable by overwhelming it. The distributed version does it from thousands of sources at once. Usually a botnet of hijacked devices, often poorly secured IoT gadgets. It's a flash mob of 10,000 people crowding into a small restaurant so real customers can't get through the door. Nobody broke in. The doorway is jammed. It attacks availability. There are flavors. Volutric attacks flood the target with raw bandwidth. Now tens of terabits per second. Protocol attacks exhaust connection tables. Application layer attacks send legitimate looking requests that are expensive to answer. And reflection and amplification attacks forge the victim's address on small queries.

So innocent servers fire much bigger replies at the victim. It's like mailing a thousand tiny postcards to catalog companies with the victim's address on them and the victim's mailbox drowns in cataloges. The defense lives out in a network. Use a content delivery network or scrubbing service to absorb floods upstream rate limiting and cloud capacity that scales and have an incident plan. Second, protocol downgrade. When two systems connect securely, they negotiate the strongest encryption they both support. A downgrade attack interferes, so both settle for an old, weak version the attacker can break. It's two spies agreeing on a code while a third person whispers to each he only knows the old code.

Use that one. A related trick is called SSL stripping which pushes a browser to plain HTTP instead of HTTPS. Defenses are to disable old protocol versions, refuse weak options, and use HSTS, which tells browsers to only ever talk to a site securely. Third, rogue devices. A rogue access point is a wireless access point someone plugged in without approval. Often it's a well-meaning employee with a cheap router, but it skips your controls either way. A nastier cousin is the evil twin, a malicious access point that copies the name of a legitimate Wi-Fi network, so devices connect to it by mistake. It's a fake kiosk next to the real coffee shop with an identical sign.

Defenses are 802.1x, wireless intrusion prevention, lock down switch ports, and regular scans and inventory. Fourth, sniffing, which means recording traffic as it passes. It's reading postcards in transit. If the message is plain text, anyone who catches it can read it. The defense is to encrypt in transit everywhere. Fifth, spoofing, which means forging an identity or address. It's a fake return address on an envelope. IP addresses and hardware addresses can both be spoofed. And ARP spoofing lies to devices on a local network about which hardware address belongs to which IP address. So traffic is sent to the attacker. It's like a mail room that updates its directory based on any note slipped under the door.

Defenses are filtering at the edge, dynamic ARP inspection and port security on switches, and strong authentication. Sixth, on path attacks, formerly called man in the middle. The attacker sits in the middle of a conversation, relaying messages between two people who think they're talking directly. They can read and change anything. It's a dishonest postal worker who opens your letters, rewrites a few lines, reseals them, and delivers them. Attackers get on the path with ARP spoofing, an evil twin, or a poison DNS answer. The defense is encryption with properly validated certificates, plus VPNs on untrusted networks and never clicking through certificate warnings. And seventh, DNS attacks. DNS is the internet's phone book.

Cash poisoning tricks a resolver into storing a forged answer. Resolvers keep recent answers in a cache, like a librarian keeping a copy of the phone book at her desk. Slip a wrong number into that copy, and everyone who asks gets sent to the attacker's site even though they typed the right address. Other DNS attacks include hijacking, which changes where queries go, and tunneling, which hides stolen data inside DNS queries because that traffic is rarely blocked. The main defense is DNS sec which digitally signs records so resolvers can verify they're genuine. It proves authenticity but it doesn't encrypt anything. Add patch software filtering and monitoring of odd query patterns and multiffactor authentication on registar accounts.
