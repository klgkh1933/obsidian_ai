## 02:57 — The identity lifecycle ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CfVDAQ9FGFE&t=177s))

cycle. Every account has a life story. It starts before the first login and ends when things go right with a clean shutdown. The first stage is identity proofing. That means verifying that a person really is who they claim to be before you create their account. It's different from authentication. Authentication asks, is this the same person who signed up? Proofing asks, who is this person in the first place? Think of opening a bank account. The teller checks your government identification and compares your face to the photo before issuing a card. Companies do the same with identification documents, selfies with liveness checks, or a video call. Proofing has levels, too. The National Institute of Standards and Technology, or NIST, describes three.

From accepting what the person tells you to verifying real evidence to checking it in a supervised in-person setting. Higher risk accounts deserve stronger proofing. A word of warning. Proofing matters most at the weakest door, and that's often the help desk. Attackers who can't beat your MFA will phone support, claim to be locked out, and sweet-talk a reset. Deepfake voices make this easier. A help desk that resets credentials without proofing the caller has handed over the keys. The second stage is provisioning. That's creating an account and granting the right access when someone joins or changes roles, ideally triggered automatically by human resources record. The third stage is permissions assignment and its implications.

Assign through roles and groups, not person by person. Follow least privilege, the minimum access needed for the job. Expect inheritance because permissions flow down from folders to subfolders and up from groups to members, and an explicit deny normally beats an allow. And watch both directions. Too many permissions means a compromised account does more damage. Too few means people can't work, and frustrated people find workarounds like emailing files to personal accounts. Here's the quiet danger. Over a few years, someone moves from finance to sales to operations, and never loses the old permissions. That slow pile-up is called privilege creep. An attacker who steals that account inherits all of it. Here's a quick tip.

Think of three moments in an account's life: joiner, mover, and leaver. Movers are the tricky ones because they need new access and must lose the old. Privilege creep is what happens when nobody handles the mover step. The fourth stage is the access review, sometimes called recertification. It's a scheduled check where managers or system owners confirm that each person still needs the access they have. It catches privilege creep, forgotten contractors, and accounts nobody remembers creating. And the last stage is deprovisioning. That's removing or disabling access when someone leaves or changes jobs. This is the step everyone forgets, and it's the one attackers exploit. An orphan account still active after its owner left is an unlocked backdoor with no one watching it.

On the exam, an employee left the company last month, but their account still works is almost always a deprovisioning failure. The fix is timely disabling, ideally automated and tied to human resources records.

## 06:34 — Account types and break-glass accounts ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CfVDAQ9FGFE&t=394s))

Now, let's move on to account types. Not every account is a person sitting at a keyboard. User accounts belong to ordinary people for everyday work with the standard permissions of their role and nothing more. Privileged accounts can change settings, create other accounts, or reach sensitive systems. They come in two flavors. Global privileged accounts hold power across an entire environment, like a domain administrator in a Windows network or a global administrator in a cloud tenant. They're the crown jewels. Local privileged accounts have administrator power on a single machine or application. Smaller blast radius, but still dangerous, especially if every laptop shares the same local administrator password. Service accounts are used by applications, scripts, and background services, not by people.

They often get ignored. Nobody changes the password, nobody notices if it leaks, and the credentials end up hardcoded in scripts. That's how stale, unmanaged credentials become an attack surface. Third-party accounts belong to outsiders, like vendors, contractors, and managed service providers. Give them only what the contract requires, tie each one to an internal sponsor, set an expiration date, require MFA, and monitor them closely. And emergency access accounts, often called break glass accounts, exist for the day normal access fails, like when your identity provider is down. Keep them few with long random passwords stored offline. Alert loudly on any use, test them on a schedule, and change their credentials after every use.

Break glass is named after the red fire alarm boxes you smash in a real emergency. If the glass breaks, everyone should hear it. Two more ideas belong here. The least privilege access account approach gives each administrator two accounts. One is a normal account for email and browsing, the other is a separate admin account used only for administrative tasks. If the daily account gets fished, the attacker lands somewhere boring. And for service accounts in Windows environments, there's a purpose-built fix. A group managed service account, or gMSA, is an active directory account that Windows manages for you. It generates a long random password, rotates it automatically every 30 days by default, and let several servers share one identity.

No human ever knows the password, so it can't end up on a sticky note or in a script. It's the service account version of a self-changing lock. Here's a quick tip. If a question asks how to stop a service account's password from going stale or being shared around, think gMSA. If it asks how to stop a departed person's account lingering, that's deprovisioning.

## 09:26 — Multi-factor authentication ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CfVDAQ9FGFE&t=566s))

Now, let's move on to multi-factor authentication. To prove you are who you claim, you present one or more authentication factors. A factor is a category of evidence. Three carry the exam, something you know, like a password or a personal identification number. Something you have, like a phone running an authenticator app, a hardware token, or a smart card. And something you are, your biometrics, like a fingerprint or a face scan. MFA stands for multi-factor authentication. The whole point is combining factors from different categories, so stealing one piece isn't enough. Imagine a vault that needs a key, a badge swipe, and your fingerprint. A thief who picks your pocket for the key still can't get in.

Three locks, three categories, one very hard door. A word of warning, two passwords is not multi-factor authentication. Neither is a password plus a security question, because both are something you know. The exam counts a password and a personal identification number as a fake MFA answer. One more note. You'll also see location and typing behavior used as extra signals when a system decides whether a login looks risky. Older study material list them as extra factors. They aren't among the objectives items, so treat them as context a system can weigh, not factors you'll be asked to count. Now the specific pieces the exam names. A hard token is a physical device, like a key fob that shows a rotating code, a smart card, or a security key you plug in and touch.

Malware on your laptop can't casually copy it. The tradeoffs are cost and that people lose them. A soft token is the software version. An authenticator app on your phone that generates codes or receives approval prompts. It's free and convenient. The tradeoff is that a phone can be lost or infected, and a tired user can be tricked into approving a prompt they didn't start. A one-time password is a code that works once and then dies. A time-based one-time password changes on a clock, typically every 30 seconds, the rotating six-digit number your authenticator app shows. A stolen code is nearly useless a minute later. Here's a quick tip. An app code or hardware token beats a text message code because text messages can be intercepted or redirected through a subscriber identity swap.

If a question asks for the most secure second factor, lean toward a hardware token or authenticator app. Biometrics use a physical trait like a fingerprint, face, or iris. The false rejection rate measures how often the right person gets locked out. The false acceptance rate measures how often the wrong person gets in, and that's the more dangerous error. The crossover error rate is where the two are equal, and a lower number means a more accurate system. And you can't change your fingerprint if it leaks, which is why good systems keep biometric data on the device. And a backup code is a one-time recovery code you receive when you set up MFA.

If you lose your phone, you can use one to get back in. Each works once. Treat them like a spare house key, stored in a password manager or printed, and never in a plain note on the same phone. The key thing to remember is this. MFA only counts when the factors come from different categories, and not all second factors are equally strong. A phishing-resistant method, like a hardware security key or a passkey, beats an app code, which beats a text message.

## 13:10 — Password concepts and NIST guidance ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CfVDAQ9FGFE&t=790s))

Now, let's move on to password concepts. Here are the classic best practices the exam knows. Length. Longer is stronger. A long passphrase beats a short jumble of symbols. Complexity. The classic rule requires a mix of uppercase, lowercase, numbers, and symbols. Reuse. Never reuse a password across sites because when one site is breached, attackers try the same login everywhere else. Expiration. The classic rule forces a change after a set time, like 90 days. Age. Maximum age is the expiration deadline. Minimum age is how long a password must stay in place before it can be changed again, which stops people from cycling through their history back to a favorite old password. Compromised credential monitoring means checking passwords against lists of known breached credentials and forcing a reset on a match.

Account auditing means regularly reviewing accounts for dormant logins, shared accounts, and excessive permissions. And a policy report shows how your accounts measure up against the password policy, who's non-compliant and where settings are weak. The objectives name it without defining it, so that's the most likely meaning. Now, a word of warning about the real world versus the exam. Current NIST guidance, publication 863B, revision four, says several things. Require at least 15 characters for a password used alone. Allow long passphrases. Drop mandatory complexity rules and routine expiration. Block known breached passwords. And force a change only when you suspect compromise. The exam objectives still list complexity, expiration, and age, so learn them as classic policy tools.

But when a question says current best practice or names NIST, pick length, block lists, MFA, and reset on compromise over forced 90-day changes. A password manager is an app that generates, stores, and fills in a long unique password for every account, locked behind one strong master password and MFA. It also helps defeat fishing because it fills credentials only on the real site's address. The trade-off is concentration of risk, so protect the vault carefully.

## 15:33 — Passkeys and passwordless sign-in ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CfVDAQ9FGFE&t=933s))

Passwordless means signing in without a password at all. The leading example is a passkey built on FIDO2 and web authentication standards. When you create a passkey, your device makes a matched pair of cryptographic keys. The public key goes to the site. The private key stays on your device and never leaves it. To sign in, the site sends a challenge. You unlock your device with a fingerprint, face, or personal identification number, checked locally and never sent anywhere. And your device signs the challenge. Nobody can steal a password from the website because the site never had one to lose. Passkeys are phishing-resistant because the signature is tied to the real site's domain.

So, a fake login page collects nothing that works. Some passkeys are device-bound. Others sync through a cloud account, which is convenient but moves some of your security to that account. A final warning. Going passwordless moves the attack to account recovery. If a passkey-protected account can be reset with a text message and a few personal facts, attackers will use that door.

## 16:41 — The six access control models ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CfVDAQ9FGFE&t=1001s))

Now, let's move on to access control models. Six show up in the objectives, and telling them apart is a dependable source of points. First, role-based access control, RBAC. Permissions attached to a role, and people get access by being assigned to that role. Picture a hospital. Every nurse gets ward access because they hold the nurse role. Hire a new nurse, drop them into the role, and they inherit the whole permission set instantly. Second, rule-based access control. It applies if-then conditions written by an administrator, and they apply the same way to everyone. A firewall rule that only accepts connections from one approved address range or a system that denies logins from outside the country is rule-based.

A word of warning. Both role-based and rule-based get abbreviated RBAC in different study materials. Don't trust the letters. If the deciding factor is a person's job, it's role-based. If it's a triggered condition, it's rule-based. If it's the clock, it's time-based. Third, DAC, discretionary access control. The owner of a resource decides who else gets in. It's the model behind sharing a document from your own drive. Flexible and friendly, but risky because a well-meaning owner can hand out access they shouldn't. Fourth, MAC, mandatory access control. This is the strict one. The system enforces access based on classification labels, and ordinary users cannot override it. You're cleared to a level, say secret, data carries a label, and you only reach data at or below your clearance.

This is the model of military and intelligence systems. MAC here is not a hardware address, the physical address burned into a network card. Read it as the clearance and label model. Fifth, time-based access control. It allows access only during set windows. A cashier's account that works only during store hours is time-based. It shrinks the window attackers have, and a 3:00 in the morning login from an account that never works nights stands out. And sixth, just-in-time access. It grants elevated permissions only when they're needed for a limited period, and then takes them back automatically. An engineer asks for admin access for a 30-minute task, someone approves it, and the rights disappear when the clock runs out.

Time-based and just-in-time sound alike, so separate them this way. Time based is a recurring schedule like 9:00 to 5:00 on weekdays. Just in time is on demand and temporary. This task, this approval, 30 minutes. One more note. Newer cloud products often decide access using many attributes at once like device health and location. That's called attribute based access control. It isn't named in the objectives, so treat it as background. Access management is the day-to-day discipline of controlling who can reach what. It starts with authentication and it's governed by policies in two families. Logical or technical policies are rules the systems enforce like password settings, account lockout, and MFA requirements. Administrative or business policies are rules people follow, written down and approved like who may request access and how often access gets reviewed.

When a question asks which control enforces something automatically, it's technical. When it asks about approval workflows or written procedures, it's administrative. Now let's move on to single sign on and

You authenticate once and that trusted session opens all your connected apps. Think of a theme park wristband. You get verified once at the gate, then it gets you onto every ride. ## 21:17 — SAML, OAuth, OIDC, LDAP, Kerberos ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CfVDAQ9FGFE&t=1277s))

Five protocols make this work. First, SAML, Security Assertion Markup Language. It's the workhorse of enterprise browser SSO. SAML passes a signed assertion from your identity provider to the app vouching that you're authenticated. Think of a sealed letter of introduction the app trusts because of who signed it. Second, OAuth, Open Authorization, and the last word is the key. OAuth is about authorization, granting one app limited permission to act on your behalf at another without handing over your password. The app receives a limited access token, not your password. It's a valet key. It starts the car, but won't open the trunk. Third, OIDC, OpenID Connect. It's a OAuth says what an app may do, not who you are.

OIDC adds a verified here's who this user actually is. The objectives don't name it, so treat it as helpful background. Here's a quick tip. SAML is enterprise browser SSO. OAuth is delegated authorization. OIDC is OAuth plus identity. Fourth, LDAP, Lightweight Directory Access Protocol. It's the protocol for querying a directory of users, groups, and resources. Think of a giant company phone book that apps consults to ask who is this person and what groups are they in. Microsoft's Active Directory speaks LDAP. Plain LDAP sends data unprotected, so real deployments wrap it in TLS. And fifth, Kerberos, the ticket-based authentication protocol at the heart of Active Directory. You authenticate once, receive a ticket, and present that ticket to prove you already checked in.

It's the valet stub. It isn't on the objectives list, but it powers Windows sign-on. Tickets expire, and a drifting clock can break logins. Let's review what we covered in this lesson. I I am answers who are you, what may you do, and what did you do with a life cycle of proofing, provisioning, review, and deprovisioning. Know your account types: user, privileged, service, third party, and emergency access, and use a GMSA for Windows service accounts. Real MFA combines factors from different categories, and passkeys are the fishing-resistant passwordless direction. The exam knows length, complexity, expiration, and age, while current NIST guidance favors long passphrases, blocklists, and resets only on compromise. Learn the six access models by their deciding factor.

Role-based is job, rule-based is condition, time-based is schedule, mandatory is labels, discretionary is owner's choice, and just-in-time is temporary approved access. An SSO logs you in once, with federation extending that trust. SAML is enterprise sign-on, OAuth is delegated authorization, OIDC is OAuth plus identity, LDAP is directory lookups, and Kerberos is tickets.
