## 00:00 — The server that started encrypting ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAmQzpzlEJ0&t=0s))

It's 2:00 in the morning, and an alert fires. A server is behaving strangely, files are encrypting, and someone on the night shift has to decide what to do first. Panic makes it worse. A plan makes it survivable. This lesson gives you the plan in the order the Security+ objectives list it, plus the evidence skills you need to figure out what really happened. ## 01:29 — Events versus incidents defined ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAmQzpzlEJ0&t=89s))

First, a definition. An event is anything observable on a system. A login, a file change, a network connection. Most events are harmless. An incident is an event, or a chain of them, that actually harms or threatens your systems or data. Incident response is the organized process of handling that trouble, from the first alert to the final report. Let's start with the stages in order. Think of a fire response. Long before any fire, the building runs drills and installs alarms. When smoke appears, someone confirms it's real. Firefighters work out what's burning, contain the blaze to one room, put it out, and reopen the building. The fire marshal tells the insurer and the neighbors.

Afterward, everyone reviews what went wrong. The CompTIA objectives list eight stages. One, preparation. Two,

identification. Three, investigation. Four, containment. Five, negotiation. Six, eradication and recovery. Seven, notification and external reporting. And eight, post incident. The key thing to remember is this order. Preparation, identification, investigation, containment, negotiation, eradication and recovery, notification and external reporting, post incident. ## 03:35 — NIST and SANS models compared ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAmQzpzlEJ0&t=215s))

classic NIST model from special publication 861 revision 2 has four phases. Preparation, detection and analysis, containment, eradication and recovery, and post incident activity. The SANS model has six steps, nicknamed PI CURL. Preparation, identification, containment, eradication, recovery, and lessons learned. And NIST's newer guidance, published in April of 2025, organizes incident response around the six functions of its Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, instead of a one-way circle of phases. Here's a quick tip. CompTIA's list is the one the exam tests, so answer from it. The other models mostly differ in what they fold together. CompTIA breaks out investigation, negotiation, and notification, which older models tuck inside other phases. If two answer choices differ only by model labels, pick the one that puts the activity at the right point in the sequence.

The logic never changes. Prepare, spot, contain, remove, restore, learn.

## 04:47 — Preparation: plans, roles, exercises ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAmQzpzlEJ0&t=287s))

Now, let's move on to preparation. This is the stage people skip and then regret. A team that prepared moves fast. A team that didn't improvises badly. Training comes first. Everyone learns to recognize and report trouble, and the response team learns tools, procedures, and evidence handling, then roles. A written incident response plan says who does what. Typical roles include an incident commander, who runs the response, technical analysts, a forensics lead, and representatives from legal, communications, human resources, and management. The team is often called a computer security incident response team. Playbooks are next. A playbook is a step-by-step script for a specific incident type, like ransomware, phishing, or a lost laptop. It's the same idea as the automation playbooks you met earlier, a written script that people follow or software runs.

Think of an airline emergency checklist. When an engine fails at 30,000 ft, pilots don't debate philosophy. They run the laminated card because someone calm wrote it in advance. Then testing, because a plan you've never tested is a guess. A tabletop exercise is a discussion-based walk-through. The team sits around a table while a facilitator describes a scenario and everyone says what they do. It's cheap, low pressure, and great for finding gaps. A simulation goes further with hands-on action. Staff actually run the tools and procedures against a realistic controlled attack. Think of a school fire drill. A tabletop is the teacher asking the class what they do if the alarm rang. A simulation is the alarm actually ringing and everyone walking to the parking lot.

A word of warning. During a serious incident, be careful how you communicate. If attackers are in your email, discussing the response over that same email tips them off. Plans often call for out-of-band communication, meaning a separate channel like phones or a dedicated app. Next is identification, which means detecting a potential incident and confirming what it is. Four sources feed it. Detection is your tools like the SIM and endpoint security plus reports from users and the help desk. An internal advisory is a notice circulated inside the organization like a security bulletin saying a phishing campaign is hitting staff. An external advisory comes from outside, a vendor bulletin, a government alert, a threat intelligence feed, or even law enforcement telling you your data turned up somewhere it shouldn't.

And threat hunting means analysts don't wait for alarms. They form a hypothesis like an attacker with stolen credentials would move between servers at night and search logs and systems for signs. Detection reacts to alerts. Hunting goes looking for what the alerts missed. Think of home security. Detection is the alarm system. An advisory is a neighbor saying burglars hit three houses on your street this week. Threat hunting is you walking the property with a flashlight because you have a hunch.

## 07:56 — Investigation and forensics ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAmQzpzlEJ0&t=476s))

Now let's move on to investigation. Once an incident is confirmed, you ask how they got in, what they touched, and how far they got. This is where digital forensics lives. Digital forensics lives the disciplined collection, preservation, and analysis of digital evidence done carefully enough to stand up in court. Four ideas dominate. Preservation, order of volatility, chain of custody, and e-discovery. Preservation means keeping evidence in its original state. A legal hold is a formal instruction to stop routine deletion once an investigation or lawsuit is likely. Hashing for integrity means you take a cryptographic hash of the original evidence. And if a copy's hash matches later, nothing changed. And you work on copies, a bit-for-bit image, never the original.

Investigators typically image a disk through a write-blocker, a device that lets them read the drive but physically prevents writes. The order of volatility says to collect the most fragile evidence first, roughly from most volatile to least. CPU registers and cache, gone in an instant. RAM, the memory, including running processes and network connections, lost when power is cut. Temporary files and swap space, data on disk, which survives a reboot. Logs on remote systems and backups. Physical configuration and network topology. And archive data and printouts, stable for years. Picture collecting evidence at a beach as the tide comes in. The footprints near the waterline wash away in minutes, so you photograph those first.

Here's a quick tip. Capture the most volatile evidence, memory, and live state before you power anything off. Once power is cut, RAM is gone for good. Chain of custody is the documented unbroken record of who handled the piece of evidence, when, why, and what they did with it. Think of a signed evidence bag passed hand to hand. Each person signs the tag. If there's an unexplained gap, a lawyer can argue the evidence was tampered with, and a judge can throw it out. The key thing to remember is that a broken chain of custody can make solid evidence legally useless. E-discovery, short for electronic discovery, is the legal process of identifying, collecting, and handing over electronically stored information, like email and chat logs, when a lawsuit demands it.

It overlaps with forensics, but answers a different question. Forensics asks, "What happened on these systems?" E-discovery asks, "Which records is the court entitled to see?" Preservation and legal holds make it possible, since you can't hand over what you already deleted.

## 10:47 — Containment: isolate, do not power off ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAmQzpzlEJ0&t=647s))

Now, let's move on to containment. Containment limits the damage while the incident is still active. You might isolate a single host, segment part of the network, disable affected accounts, or block malicious domains at the firewall. The classic mistake is pulling the power cord. It feels decisive, but powering off wipes memory, which often holds the richest evidence, and some attackers set traps that trigger on shutdown. The better move is quarantine, also called isolation. Cut the machine off from the network, so it can't spread or phone home, but leave it running, so you can capture memory. Think of quarantining a patient, not executing them. You still need to examine them. Containment comes in two speeds.

Short-term containment is the fast temporary move to stop damage right now. Long-term containment keeps the business running safely while you prepare eradication, like standing up a clean replacement server. Next is negotiation. In a ransomware or extortion incident, attackers demand payment, often threatening to leak stolen data. Negotiation is the stage

## 11:53 — Negotiation with attackers ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAmQzpzlEJ0&t=713s))

where the organization decides whether and how to communicate with them. It's a business, legal, and law enforcement decision, not an analyst's call. Paying certain sanction groups can itself be illegal, so legal counsel and law enforcement come first. Payment doesn't guarantee working decryption or deleted data. Clean tested backups may remove the need to pay at all, and many organizations hire professional negotiators and involve their cyber insurer. Here's a quick tip. If a question describes an extortion demand, the best answer almost never starts with pay immediately. Look for legal, leadership, law enforcement, and restoring from backups. Then comes eradication and recovery.

## 12:35 — Eradication and recovery ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAmQzpzlEJ0&t=755s))

Eradication removes the threat entirely. Delete the malware, close the exploited hole, remove backdoors, and reset compromised credentials. Containment stops the bleeding. Eradication removes the cause. Recovery restores systems to normal safe operation. Rebuild from known clean backups, patch, bring services back in a deliberate order, and monitor closely to make sure the attacker doesn't return. Here's how next step questions work. If analysts have confirmed ransomware spreading, you're at identification, and the next step is containment, not rebuilding servers. If analysts have removed the malware and closed the hole, you're finishing eradication, and the next step is recovery, not a lessons learned meeting, which is too early. Find your current stage, step to the one that follows.

## 13:31 — Notification and external reporting ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAmQzpzlEJ0&t=811s))

Now, let's move on to notification and external reporting. Your plan should say in advance who gets told by whom and when. Stakeholders include executives, the board, employees, partners, and your cyber insurer. Customers are usually owed clear, timely notice if their data was exposed. Law enforcement can help and often wants to hear about extortion and fraud. And mandatory reporting means laws, regulations, and contracts that require you to notify someone by a deadline. The numbers change and they're not the heart of the exam. The heart of it is knowing that deadlines exist and that legal and compliance teams must be involved early. Still, recognize the big ones. Under GDPR, the European privacy law, you notify the regulator within 72 hours of becoming aware of a breach that poses a risk to people.

Under HIPAA, the healthcare privacy rule, you notify affected individuals without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days after discovery. And breaches of 500 or more people also go to the federal health agency with the media told when more than 500 residents of one state are affected. Public companies in the United States must file a disclosure with the SEC within four business days after determining an incident is material. Every United States has its own breach law, many with deadlines of 30 to 60 days, and many requiring notice to the state to 60 days, and many requiring notice to the state attorney general. A word of warning on one more. CIRCIA, the Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act, is a 2022 law that will require covered critical infrastructure companies to report substantial incidents and ransom payments to the Federal Cybersecurity Agency.

Its details come from a rule-making process that has run for years, so check its current status before treating any deadline as binding. The same goes for every rule here. Rules get amended, so confirm the current version before relying on a number in real work. Here's a quick tip. On the exam, which rule applies is decided by the kind of data or organization. European personal data points to GDPR. Patient data points to HIPAA. A public company's material incident points to the SEC pay. Now, the post-incident stage, which is where good teams get better. It has three parts. Lessons learned is a

## 16:02 — Lessons learned and root cause ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAmQzpzlEJ0&t=962s))

meeting soon after recovery where everyone reviews what worked, what failed, and what was confusing. It should be blameless. Think of a flight crew reviewing a near miss. Nobody blames the pilot. They find the ambiguous checklist step and fix it. Root cause analysis digs past symptoms to the true underlying cause. The ransomware encrypted files, but how did it get in? An unpatched server, a fished employee, a stolen password with no MFA. The root cause is what you actually fix. And post-incident reporting, called PIR, is a written report on the timeline, what was affected, the cost, the root cause, how it was handled, and what will change. Each fix gets an owner and a deadline because lessons learned without owners becomes lessons written down and ignored.

Those improvements flow back into preparation and the loop closes.

## 17:00 — Logs and data sources for evidence ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAmQzpzlEJ0&t=1020s))

Now, let's turn to the evidence that supports an investigation. Start with log and trace data types because different logs answer different questions. Access and accounting logs show who got in and what they did once inside. Logical access covers logins and permission changes, and physical access covers badge readers and door logs. Accounting logs record what an identity did or used, like when a session started and stopped, which commands it ran, and which resources it touched. That's separate from authentication logs, which only record whether the login itself worked. Device logs come from hardware like routers and printers. Server logs show what happened on a specific server. Application logs show what happened inside an app.

Authentication logs record logins, failures, and lockouts. Communication logs record email, chat, and voice traffic. Audit logs record email, chat, and voice traffic. Audit logs record who changed what, especially security-relevant changes. Endpoint logs show process launches and file changes on laptops and servers. Network logs come from firewalls, routers, and name servers. And metadata is data about data, like file creation times, authors, and email headers, which often proves who touched a file and when, even when the content is gone. Here's a quick tip. Match the log to the question. Did this account log in from somewhere strange? That's authentication. Who changed that firewall rule? That's audit. Who entered the server room at 3:00 in the morning?

That's physical access. Beyond logs, investigators draw on many data sources. Vulnerability scans show what weaknesses existed before the incident. Automated reports give scheduled summaries. Security tools like firewalls, intrusion detection, and endpoint detection add their own output. Dashboards give live views, great for triage, but they change constantly, so capture and export if you need a record. Surveillance footage confirms who physically entered a space or plugged in a device, and it needs retention and chain of custody, too. Then there's NetFlow and IPFix. NetFlow is a record of network conversations, who talked to whom, on which ports, when, and how much data, but not the contents. IPfix, Internet Protocol Flow Information Export, is the open standardized flow format based on NetFlow.

Flow data is lightweight and perfect for spotting data theft or regular beaconing to an attacker's server. Think of the phone bill. It shows who called whom, how long, and how often. A packet capture is the call recording. It's a full copy of the traffic collected with tools like Wireshark, detailed but huge to store, and unreadable if the traffic was encrypted. Logs are only useful if you can trust them, and attackers know it. They delete or edit logs to hide their tracks. So, you protect file and log integrity. Hash files and logs to detect any change. Forward logs immediately to a central, access-restricted system, so the attacker can't erase the only copy.

Use write-once storage. Use file integrity monitoring, which alerts when critical files change, and keep clocks synchronized so timelines line up.

## 20:22 — System images and memory dumps ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAmQzpzlEJ0&t=1222s))

When you need to preserve the state of a machine, you capture a system image, and three kinds matter. A memory dump captures the contents of RAM at that moment, ideal for running malware, encryption keys, and live connections. It comes first because of the order of volatility. A bit-level copy, also called a forensic image, captures every bit of a drive, including deleted files and unused space. That's far more than a file copy, and it's usually made through a write blocker and verified with a hash. A snapshot is a point-in-time copy of a virtual machine or cloud volume. It's fast and convenient, but follow your organization's policy on whether it's enough for evidence, and hash it like anything else.

The key thing to remember is this: capture memory first, then make a bit level copy, prove integrity with a hash, and work on the copy, not the original. An investigation also touches more than the security team. Human resources is essential when an employee is involved, whether as a suspect, a victim, or a witness. They guide interviews and fair handling. Accounts is a stakeholder. CompTIA lists without defining. The most likely meaning is an accounting needed when money moves, like fraud, fake invoices, business email compromise, ransom payments, and insurance claims. They can freeze or recall payments and track the incident's cost. And legal advises on privilege, preservation, regulatory duties, and law enforcement contact.

A word of warning. Don't investigate an employee in secret from human resources and legal. Accessing someone's mailbox without the right approvals can create legal trouble for you, even when your suspicion is right. Finally, log passing techniques. Raw logs are walls of text. Passing breaks each entry into labeled fields, like timestamp, user, source address, action, and result, so you can search, sort, and count. You search and filter with tools like grep or your SIM's query language. You use regular expressions, compact patterns that describe text, to pull fields out of messy lines. You split on delimiters or read structured formats like JSON, where fields already have names. You normalize, converting different formats and time zones into one common layout, usually coordinated universal time.

You aggregate and count, finding the top source addresses or the most failed logins, so outliers stand out. And you correlate by time, lining up events from several sources into one timeline. Picture a failed login line from one server. Count that same source address across 50 servers and it jumps out as the machine hammering every one of them. Here's a quick tip. Normalized timestamps are the secret weapon. If one system logs in local time and another in universal time, your timeline lies.

## 23:26 — Review and key takeaways ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAmQzpzlEJ0&t=1406s))

Let's review what we covered in this lesson. Learn the stages in CompTIA's order: preparation, identification, investigation, containment, negotiation, eradication, and recovery, notification and external reporting, and post-incident. Other models, like the classic NIST phases and the SANS steps, bundle the same work under different labels. Prepare with training, roles, playbooks, table-top exercises, and simulations. Identify through detection, advisories, and threat hunting. Investigate with forensics. Preserve evidence, collect in order of volatility, and keep the chain of custody unbroken. Contain by isolating, not by pulling the plug, and meet mandatory reporting deadlines. And finish with lessons learned, root cause analysis, and a post-incident report using the right logs, flow data, packet captures, and system images, and involving human resources, accounts, and legal.

## 24:30 — Quick check questions ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mAmQzpzlEJ0&t=1470s))

Now, here are a few quick checks to lock this in. First question. A scenario says analysts have just confirmed ransomware actively spreading across the network. What is the next step? Containment. You isolate the infected machines to stop the spread. You don't jump ahead to recovery while the fire is still burning. Second question. You need to contain a a machine, but also preserve the evidence in its memory, should you pull the power cord? No. Pulling power wipes the RAM, and it can tip off the attacker. Isolate the machine from the network, but leave it running. Third question. In the order of volatility, what do you collect first? The data on the hard drive or the contents of memory?

RAM vanishes the moment power is cut, while disk data survives a reboot. So, you grab the fragile evidence first. You've now handled the crisis. The last piece of running security day-to-day is stepping back to the big picture. The policies, risk decisions, and oversight that govern the whole program.
