## 01:35 — What cryptography actually does ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xns4tNjM-yg&t=95s))

Let's start with what cryptography actually does. Cryptography is the practice of scrambling information so only the right people can read it or verify it. That's the whole game. You take readable data, called plain text, and you turn it into unreadable data, called cipher text, using a secret ingredient called a key. The key is the star of the show. The scrambling method, the algorithm, is usually public knowledge. That surprises people. Shouldn't you hide the recipe? No. A good algorithm stays safe even when everyone knows how it works as long as the key stays secret. Think of a padlock design. The whole world can know how a padlock is built, but only you hold the key that opens yours.

Here's a quick tip. When a question asks about protecting the content of data, it's asking about encryption, which is confidentiality. When it asks about proving data didn't change, it's asking about hashing, which is integrity. Sorting the question into one of those two buckets solves half the battle. Now, let's move on to symmetric encryption.

## 02:46 — Symmetric encryption ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xns4tNjM-yg&t=166s))

Symmetric encryption uses the same key to lock and unlock the data. Both sides share one secret key. You encrypt with it, you decrypt with it, and you're done. Picture a single house key that you and your roommate both carry. Either of you can lock the front door, and either of you can open it because it's the same key. That shared key is both the strength and the weakness. It's fast, but you have to get a copy of that key to the other person safely first, and that's the hard part. Symmetric encryption is fast, which is why it does the heavy lifting for bulk data. The big name to know is AES, the Advanced Encryption Standard, the modern gold standard.

You'll see it written as AES-128, AES-256, or AES-256, where the number is the key length in bits. Longer key, harder to crack. You should also recognize the old-timers. DES, the Data Encryption Standard, is long retired because its key is far too short. Its patched successor, Triple DES, hung around for years, but NIST, the United States standards agency, no longer allows it for new encryption as of the end of 2023. If an exam scenario offers you DES or Triple DES versus AES, then AES is almost always the better answer. A word of warning. Symmetric encryption's core problem is key distribution. How do you hand someone the shared secret without an eavesdropper grabbing it in transit?

That single question is what asymmetric encryption was invented to solve. So, let's turn to that next.

## 04:25 — Asymmetric encryption ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xns4tNjM-yg&t=265s))

Asymmetric encryption uses a matched pair of keys. There's a public key you can hand to anyone, and a private key you guard with your life. What one key locks, only the other can unlock. Here's the mental picture that makes it click. Imagine a mailbox on a public street. Anyone walking by can drop a letter through the slot. That's the public key doing the locking, but only you have the private key to open the mailbox and read what's inside. You can print your mailbox slot on a billboard, and it's still secure because dropping mail in is not the same as taking mail out. That solves the key distribution nightmare. You never have to secretly share anything.

You publish your public key openly. People use it to encrypt messages to you, and only your private key can decrypt them. So, what's the trade-off? Asymmetric encryption is slow and computationally heavy. You wouldn't want to encrypt a 2-hour video this way. The algorithms to know are RSA, named for its inventors Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman, which is the classic, and ECC, which is elliptic curve cryptography. ECC gives you the same security with much smaller keys. It's popular on phones and low-power devices precisely because small keys mean less work. That leads us to the best of both worlds. So, symmetric is fast, but hard to share keys. Asymmetric is easy to share keys, but slow.

## 05:56 — Hybrid encryption in your browser ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xns4tNjM-yg&t=356s))

hybrid encryption, and it's how your browser secures nearly every website you visit. Here's the trick, step-by-step. First, use asymmetric encryption to share one small secret. Your browser and the website use asymmetric math, their public and private keys or a Diffie-Hellman-style exchange, which the next lesson explains, to safely agree on a fresh symmetric key. Only that little key goes through the slow, secure step. Second, switch to symmetric encryption for the actual conversation. Now that both sides share the same symmetric key, they use fast AES to encrypt the real traffic, the web pages, the passwords, all of it. Third, throw the symmetric key away when done. The session ends, the key is discarded, and next time you get a brand new one.

The key thing to remember is this. Asymmetric encryption solves key distribution. Symmetric encryption does the fast bulk work. Real-world systems combine them into hybrid encryption. If you remember one sentence about crypto for the exam, make it that one.

## 09:06 — Hashing: a one-way fingerprint ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xns4tNjM-yg&t=546s))

far has been about confidentiality. Hashing is about integrity, proving data hasn't changed. A hash function takes any input of any size, and produces a fixed-length string of gibberish called a hash or a digest. Feed it the same input, and you always get the same output. Change even one character of the input, and the output changes completely. Here's the crucial part. It only goes one way. You cannot run the hash backward to recover the original data. Think of a blender. You can turn strawberries and banana into a smoothie in seconds, but no amount of effort turns that smoothie back into whole fruit. Hashing is that blender. Easy one direction, impossible the other.

So, what's it good for? Verifying integrity. Download a file, hash it, and compare your hash to the one the publisher listed. If they match, the file wasn't corrupted or tampered with. If a single byte changed, the hashes won't match, and you'll know. The algorithms to recognize here are SHA, the secure hash algorithm, specifically the SHA-2 and SHA-3 families, which are current. MD5, the message digest five, is old and broken, so treat it as legacy. SHA-1 is the middle child. Researchers have produced real collisions, and NIST has told everyone to be off it by the end of 2030. If a scenario pits MD5 or SHA-1 against SHA-256, then SHA-256 wins. The next lesson looks at choosing between these and at hashing passwords.

A word of warning. Hashing does not provide confidentiality. It doesn't hide your data. It fingerprints it. The exam loves to bait you here. If a question asks how to keep data secret, hashing is the wrong answer. Hashing proves data is unchanged, nothing more.

## 11:07 — Salting stored passwords ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xns4tNjM-yg&t=667s))

Now, let's talk about salting. Passwords are stored as hashes, not as plain text, so a stolen database doesn't hand over everyone's password directly. But attackers got clever. They built rainbow tables, which are giant precomputed lists of common passwords and their hashes. Steal the hashes, look them up in the table, and out pop the passwords. The defense is a salt. A salt is a random value added to each password before hashing. Because every user gets a different random salt, two people with the same password end up with completely different hashes. The attacker's precomputed table becomes useless because it was built without knowing your specific salt. Think of a salt like adding a unique secret ingredient to each person's recipe before you blend it.

Even if two people order the same smoothie, yours has a pinch of something no cookbook lists, so the attacker's cookbook of known smoothies can't identify it. One more detail for real systems. A plain fast hash, even a salted one, lets an attacker test guesses very quickly. So, real password storage pairs the salt with a deliberately slow algorithm. The next lesson covers those. Here's a quick tip. Salt defeats rainbow tables. Always salt stored passwords. On the exam, if you see stored passwords plus rainbow tables in the same scenario, then adding a salt is the answer they're fishing for. The next concept is digital signatures. Encryption hides data. ## 12:45 — Digital signatures: the wax seal ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xns4tNjM-yg&t=765s))

something new. They prove who created a message and that it wasn't altered. That's authenticity and integrity together, which adds up to non-repudiation, the property that a sender can't later deny they sent something. Signatures flip asymmetric encryption around. Instead of encrypting with the recipient's public key, you sign with your own private key. Here's the flow. First, hash the message. You run the message through a hash function to get its digest. Second, encrypt that hash with your private key. That encrypted hash is the digital signature attached to the message. Third, the recipient verifies with your public key. They decrypt the signature using your public key, hash the message themselves, and compare. If the two hashes match, the message came from you and wasn't changed.

Only your private key could have created a signature that your public key unlocks. That's the proof of identity, and because a hash is involved, any tampering breaks the match. Picture a wax seal on an old letter. The unique imprint proves it came from a specific person since only they own that seal ring, and an unbroken seal proves nobody opened and rewrote the letter along the way. A digital signature is that wax seal made of math. If you want to go a little deeper, notice the mirror image. To keep a message secret, you encrypt with a recipient's public key, so only their private key can open it. To sign a message, you use your own private key, so anyone with your public key can verify it.

Confidentiality uses the recipient's keys. Signing uses the sender's keys. If you can hold both directions in your head, you understand asymmetric crypto better than most. In both directions, the private key never leaves its owner. Strictly speaking, real signature algorithms don't literally encrypt the hash, but that's the picture the exam uses. ## 16:18 — The chain of trust: root to leaf ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xns4tNjM-yg&t=978s))

browser doesn't personally know every certificate authority. Instead, it trusts a small number of root certificate authorities whose certificates ship built into your operating system and browser. ## 19:11 — Worked example: contract to Priya ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xns4tNjM-yg&t=1151s))
