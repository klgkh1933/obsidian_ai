## 02:27 — Confidentiality: keeping secrets secret ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RZnprQW-3Ac&t=147s))

first leg is confidentiality. Keeping secrets, confidentiality means only the people who are supposed to see information can see it. Everyone else is locked out. Your medical records, your bank balance, a company's secret product plans, all of that should stay confidential. Think of a sealed envelope. The letter inside is meant for one person. As long as the envelope stays sealed and reaches the right hands, confidentiality holds. The moment a stranger steams it open and reads it, confidentiality is broken. How do we protect it in the real world? Mostly with two tools. The first is encryption, which scramles data into gibberish that only the right key can unscramble. You'll go deep on this later in the course, but the headline is simple.

Encryption is the workhorse of confidentiality. The second is access control, which decides who's allowed near the data in the first place. Passwords, permissions, and locked doors all serve confidentiality.

## 03:30 — Integrity: authorised vs unauthorised change ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RZnprQW-3Ac&t=210s))

The second leg is integrity, keeping information trustworthy. Integrity means information stays accurate and unaltered, unless someone with permission changes it on purpose. If a number in your bank account changed on its own overnight, that's an integrity failure and a terrifying one. Here's the subtle part that the exam loves. Integrity is not about preventing all change. It's about preventing unauthorized change. Files get updated, records get corrected. That's normal and fine. Integrity asks, "Was this change made by the right person for the right reason? And can we prove nothing else was tampered with?" Here's a quick tip. Integrity is about unauthorized change, not all change. When a scenario says data was modified, ask who did it and whether they were allowed to.

Authorized edits are fine. That's the whole point of having editable data. Picture a signed contract where every sheet is initialed. If someone later swaps out the third sheet, the missing initial gives it away. The initials don't stop editing. They make unauthorized editing detectable. That's integrity in a nutshell. The main technical tool here is hashing, which you'll meet properly later in the course. A hash is like a tamper evidence seal on a package. Run the data through a hashing formula and you get a short fingerprint. Change even one character and the fingerprint changes completely. So you instantly know something was altered. The third

## 05:01 — Availability: keeping systems working ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RZnprQW-3Ac&t=301s))

leg is availability. Keeping things working. Availability means the information and systems are there when authorized people need them. A vault that keeps your money perfectly safe but only opens once a year has failed at availability. Security that nobody can use isn't security. It's just an obstacle. This is the leg people forget because it doesn't sound like security. But think about it. If attackers can't steal your data, their next best move is to stop you from reaching it. That's exactly what a ransomware attack does, locking your files until you pay. And what a denial of service attack does, flooding a system until it collapses under the load. We protect availability with things like the following: redundancy and backups, so a failure or attack doesn't wipe you out. load balancing and failover, so if one server dies, another picks up instantly, and patching and maintenance, so systems don't crash from known bugs.

Remember the dimmer switch idea from the previous lesson? Confidentiality and availability constantly pull against each other. Crank confidentiality to the max, locking data behind endless approvals, and availability suffers. The art is balancing all three legs, not maxing one. ## 06:23 — The DAD triad: disclosure, alteration, destruction ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RZnprQW-3Ac&t=383s))

The evil twin, the dad triad. For every goal in the CIA triad, there's an opposite failure. Line them up and you get the dad triad. Disclosure, alteration, and destruction. The dad triad is what happens when the CIA triad fails. Think of it as three cause and effect pairs. When confidentiality fails, you get disclosure, which means a secret got exposed. When integrity fails, you get alteration, which means data got changed without permission. And when availability fails, you get destruction, which means access to data or systems got wiped out. If you can name the failure, you can name the goal that was supposed to prevent it. And that's a fast way to reason through exam questions.

So, the dad triad isn't a second thing to memorize. It's the same three ideas viewed from the failure side. Here's a quick tip. When a scenario describes something bad happening, name the Dad Triod failure first. That's disclosure. Then flip it to the CIA goal that was broken. So, confidentiality failed. ## 07:38 — Authenticity, non-repudiation and accountability ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RZnprQW-3Ac&t=458s))

non-repudiation, and accountability. The CIA triad is the foundation, but modern security stacks a few more goals on top. Three matter most for the exam. Authenticity means something is genuinely what or who it claims to be. An authentic email really did come from the sender it names. An authentic file really is the untampered original. Authenticity answers the question, is this the real deal or a forgery? Non-repudiation is a mouthful. So, let's break it down. Repudiate means to deny or reject. Encryption serves confidentiality, hashing serves integrity, and digital signatures serve authenticity and non-repudiation. Keep that mapping in your pocket. Accountability is the ability to trace an action back to whoever did it. If every action in a system is logged and tied to an identity, people are accountable because there's a record. ## 09:52 — AAA: authentication, authorisation, accounting ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RZnprQW-3Ac&t=592s))

quietly powers almost every access decision and securitya which stands for authentication, authorization and accounting. These three work as a pipeline and the order matters. Picture getting into a nightclub. It maps perfectly. First, authentication is proving who you are. At the door, you show your ID. The bouncer checks it's really you. In computing, this is your password, your fingerprint, your security token. Authentication answers, who are you? Second, authorization is what you're allowed to do once you're in. Your wristband says general admission or VIP. General admission can't walk into the VIP lounge. In computing, this is your permissions. Which files you can open, which systems you can touch. Authorization answers, what are you allowed to do?

Third, accounting is the record of what happened. The club logs that you entered at 10 at night and left at 2:00 in the morning. In computing, this is the audit log tracking every action you took. Accounting answers, what did you actually do? Notice the sequence. You can't be authorized before you're authenticated because the system has to know who you are before it can decide what you get. And accounting runs across the whole visit, recording it all. Authentication, then authorization, then accounting logging the lot. A word of warning, do not confuse authentication with authorization. This is one of the most tested traps on the whole exam. Authentication is who you are.

Authorization is what you may do. You are authenticated once when you prove your identity, then authorized many times. Each time you try to access something, consider the question carefully. If it's about proving identity, that's authentication. If it's about permissions, that's authorization. You'll often see them written as orth N and or Z to keep them straight. How does AAA support the CIA triad? Directly. Authentication and authorization together enforce confidentiality by keeping unauthorized people away from data they shouldn't see. They enforce integrity by making sure only permitted users can change things. and accounting supports accountability and non-repudiation because the logs prove who did what. AAA is the machinery. The CIA triad is the goal it serves.

The key thing to remember is this. AAA is the practical engine behind the triad. Authentication proves identity. Authorization grants the right level of access and accounting records everything for later. Together they turn the abstract goals of the CIA triad into real enforcable controls. ## 13:07 — Zero trust: never trust, always verify ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RZnprQW-3Ac&t=787s))

let's plant one more idea that sits right next to AAA, zero trust. You'll hear zero trust all over the security world. So, here's the core of it. Now, the full architecture comes later in the course. Zero trust is a security approach built on one phrase, never trust, always verify. Sitting inside the company network doesn't earn you a free pass. Neither does having logged in once this morning. Four principles carry most of the weight. First, verify explicitly. Every request proves who's asking and the system weighs context, too. Is the device healthy? Is this request normal for this person? How sensitive is the thing they want? Second, verify continuously. Trust isn't a one-time stamp at the front door.

It gets rechecked as conditions change. Third, use least privilege. That means giving someone only the access they need for their job and nothing more. So a stolen account can only reach a small slice of your systems. You'll see it in action in the very next lesson. And fourth, assume breach. Design as if an attacker is already inside. That pushes you to limit how far one compromised account can reach and to log and watch everything. Picture a museum with timed entry tickets. Your ticket gets you through the front door, but each gallery has its own guard who checks it again, and the staff assume a pickpocket could be wandering around any day.

Being inside the building proves nothing. That's the zero trust attitude. Notice how neatly this lines up with AAA. Authentication happens at every request. Authorization is narrow and accounting records all of it. Zero trust is AAA taken seriously with no assumption that anyone is safe because of where they sit. A word of warning, zero trust is an approach, not a product you can buy in a box. If an answer choice treats it as a single appliance, be suspicious. Later in the course, the zero trust lesson covers the principles and moving parts and the cloud lesson shows how to apply them.
