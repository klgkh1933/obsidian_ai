## 00:00 — Governance beyond your own walls ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtfopUHES4Q&t=0s))

Your security is only as strong as the weakest company you do business with. If your payroll provider gets hacked, your employees' data is gone, no matter how good your own defenses are. Governance doesn't stop at your own front door, and this lesson is about everything that lies beyond it. In the last lesson, you learned how a security program writes down its rules and measures its risk. Now, we look outward and then inward. In this lesson, you'll learn four things. First, third-party risk, meaning how you choose, contract with, and monitor vendors. Second, compliance, privacy rights, and legal duties. Third, audits and penetration testing, meaning how someone checks that it's all real.

And fourth, security awareness training, the human side of the program. This is the rest of the 14% that domain five carries, and the vocabulary keeps coming.

## 00:55 — Choosing vendors: RFI, RFP, diligence ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtfopUHES4Q&t=55s))

Let's start with third-party risk because you inherit it. A compromised vendor becomes your breach. Start with vendor selection. Think of hiring a contractor. You don't sign with the first name you see. An expression of interest, or EOI, is a vendor raising a hand to be considered. A request for information, or RFI, asks what vendors can do with no commitment. A request for quote, or RFQ, describes exactly what you want and asks for a price. And the request for proposal, or RFP, is the big one. You describe a problem and ask for a full proposal covering approach, price, and security answers. Due diligence is the homework before you sign. Reviewing their security posture, audit reports, financial health, references, and breach history.

And conflict of interest is the trap in the process. If the person choosing the vendor has a personal stake, the choice is tainted. The fix is disclosure and stepping out of the decision.

## 01:59 — Agreements: SLA, MOU, MOA ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtfopUHES4Q&t=119s))

Now, the agreements. An SLA, a service level agreement, is the promised level of service with measurable numbers and consequences. 99.9% uptime, or you get a credit. An SLO, a service level objective, is a performance target. It's the goal a provider aims for, but on its own, it doesn't necessarily carry penalties. An MOU, a memorandum of understanding, is a less formal statement of intent to cooperate, often not legally binding. A MOA, a memorandum of agreement, is firmer, spelling out who will do what. An NDA, a non-disclosure agreement, is a confidentiality contract. An MSA, a master services agreement, is the umbrella contract setting general terms. And an SOW, a statement of work, is the specific job under that umbrella, with scope, deliverables, timeline, and price.

The master services agreement is the rules of the house, and the statement of work is the job order. A contract is the start, not the finish.

## 03:05 — Six tools to keep a vendor honest ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtfopUHES4Q&t=185s))

Six tools keep a vendor honest. The right to audit is a clause letting you, or an auditor you choose, inspect the vendor's controls. Service level monitoring tracks real uptime and response times against the agreement. A vendor registry is one central inventory of every vendor, what data and access each has, how critical it is, and who owns the relationship. Vendor assessment means reviewing vendors regularly, with the riskiest getting the deepest look. Compliance attestation means the vendor formally states, or an independent party confirms, that they meet a requirement, like a SOC 2 report. And penetration testing means requiring the vendor to be tested, or testing them yourself with written permission. Real programs have limits.

Staffing limitations and resource availability mean too few people and too little time to assess every vendor deeply, so you tear vendors by risk. The environment covers technical realities like a vendor product that only runs on an outdated system or can't connect to yours. Legal and regulatory factors and geography or jurisdiction cover which laws apply and where the vendor stores data. Financial limits and return on investment mean oversight must be worth what it costs. Vendor lock-in is the pain of leaving once you depend on a provider's proprietary tools, so plan exits early with data portability and termination terms. And assurance mechanisms are the ways you gain confidence without watching constantly. Audits, certifications, attestations, and insurance.

One more piece, when anyone tests you or you test someone else, a written rules of engagement document sets the boundaries first. Scope, allowed techniques, testing windows, who to call in an emergency, and how findings are handled paired with signed authorization. Without it, a penetration test looks like an attack.

## 05:03 — Compliance, GDPR and privacy law ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtfopUHES4Q&t=303s))

Now, compliance and privacy, the rules others impose. Compliance means meeting requirements set by laws, regulations, contracts, or industry bodies. Know what each of these guards. GDPR, the General Data Protection Regulation, is the European Union's privacy law applying to anyone handling the personal data of European residents. It can fine up to 20 million euros or 4% of worldwide turnover, whichever is higher, and breaches must be reported within 72 hours. The California Consumer Privacy Act gives California residents the right to know, delete, correct, and opt out of the sale or sharing of their data. HIPAA, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act protects PHI, protected health information. If a scenario mentions patient records, think HIPAA.

SOX, the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, is about trustworthy financial records for public companies, and it makes destroying records to obstruct an investigation a crime. The second Network and Information Security Directive, NIS2, requires risk management, supply chain security, and fast incident reporting from essential and important European organizations. Each country writes it into its own law at its own speed, so check current status. And DORA, the Digital Operational Resilience Act, has applied since January of 2025 to European financial firms and their critical technology providers, demanding incident reporting, resilience testing, and a register of every technology vendor. These rules protect PII, personally identifiable information, and PIH I. And data sovereignty means the laws of the country where data physically sits can apply to it.

Falling short hurts in six ways: reputational damage, financial penalties, legal consequences, contractual penalties, sanctions from regulators or governments, and loss of license, like losing the right to process cards. Some training is required by law or contract. Data handling training teaches how to treat each classification of data. Anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing training teaches financial staff to spot and report suspicious transactions. Anti-bribery training covers gifts, hospitality, and payments to officials. Then you have to prove people did it. An acknowledgement is a person saying, "I've read this policy and I agree to follow it." An attestation is a formal statement that something is true, like a manager attesting that access reviews were done.

Privacy laws also give individuals rights. The right to be forgotten means you can ask for your data to be erased unless a legal reason requires keeping it. Opt-in means nothing happens until you say yes, and opt-out means it happens until you say stop. Data

## 09:44 — Audits and assessments explained ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtfopUHES4Q&t=584s))

Now, audit and assessment, which is checking that it's real. An audit is like a home inspection before you buy a house. Someone qualified walks through with a checklist and tells you what's sound and what's cracked. An audit charter is the formal document, usually approved by the board's audit committee, that defines the audit function's purpose, authority, independence, and access to records. Scoping decides what a given engagement covers, which systems, processes, and time period. And frequency is how often it happens, annually, after major changes, or when a regulator requires it. A gap analysis compares where you are against where a standard says you should be and list the gaps. It's not pass or fail, it's a to-do list.

Internal engagements are done by your own people, including compliance reviews, oversight by the audit committee, and self-assessments. External engagements are done by outsiders, including formal examinations, like a bank regulator's exam. Assessments against criteria, regulatory reviews, and independent third-party audits, like the ones behind a SOC 2 report. Benchmarking compares your results against peers or a published baseline. Independent audits carry more weight than internal ones because the auditor has no stake in the answer. Auditors gather evidence in four ways. Sampling checks a representative subset, like tasting a spoonful of soup, though a sample can miss the one bad item. Questionnaires and surveys are cheap and scalable, but self-reported. Interviews reveal what really happens, though people say what they think the auditor wants to hear.

## 12:17 — Behavioural and penetration testing ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtfopUHES4Q&t=737s))

Two kinds of testing round out the list. Functional testing checks that a feature does what its requirements say, like does the login actually demand a second factor? Behavioral testing observes how a system actually behaves, including under odd or hostile conditions nobody wrote a requirement for. What does the application do with malformed input? Does it fail safely? Does an error message give away details an attacker could use? You saw a short definition of penetration testing earlier when we talked about reading the report. In audit terms, it's evidence that controls hold up against a real attempt. How much does the tester know beforehand? In a known environment, the tester gets full information, white box.

In an unknown environment, the tester gets nothing, like a real outsider, black box. In a partially known environment, some information is shared, gray box. Where does the test happen and who plays which side? A physical test means trying to get into the building by tailgating or cloning badges. Offensive means the red team attacks. Defensive means the blue team's detection and response are what's tested, and integrated means red and blue share findings in real time, the purple team approach. Reconnaissance is the information gathering phase. Passive reconnaissance never touches the target systems. It means reading public records, job postings, and social media, and it's hard to detect. Active reconnaissance interacts directly, like scanning ports, so it can be logged and noticed.

A word of warning. The split isn't about speed or aggression, it's whether you touch the target. Finally, the human element, which is security awareness. Technology can't fix a person who clicks a malicious link.

## 14:09 — Security awareness training ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtfopUHES4Q&t=849s))

Awareness training is the human control that backs up every technical one. There are four types. Initial or onboarding training gives every new hire the basics. Ongoing training provides regular refreshes. Targeted training gives extra instruction to higher-risk roles, like finance staff and administrators, and corrective training follows up with someone who failed a phishing simulation. It's coaching, not punishment. Delivery has four mechanisms. A learning management system, or LMS, hosts courses and tracks completion and scores. Self-service portals let people learn when it suits them. One-to-one delivery is coaching, and one-to-many delivery is a live session or all-hands briefing. ## 16:01 — Remote work and device hygiene ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtfopUHES4Q&t=961s))

hybrid work, like locking screens in shared spaces. Bring your own device training teaches habits. Keep the software updated. Don't jailbreak the phone and report a lost device immediately so it can be wiped. Removable media and cables matter because attackers exploit curiosity. In a USB drop, also called baiting, they scatter infected thumb drives in a parking lot and wait for someone to plug one in. A malicious cable looks like an ordinary charger but hides a tiny computer that can inject keystrokes or steal data. So, never plug in an unknown drive or cable. Use your own charger and use a data blocker, an adapter that passes power but no data at public charging stations.

Training also covers situational awareness, operational security, which means not oversharing details that help attackers, and emerging topics like deepfake voices, QR code phishing, and pasting company data into public artificial intelligence tools. A security culture ties it together. It's the everyday habit of taking security seriously and it's what makes an employee report a suspicious email instead of quietly deleting it. And for the exam, when users keep falling for phishing despite good technical controls, the best answer is usually awareness training, not another firewall. The gap is human, so the fix is human.

## 18:03 — Quick check questions ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GtfopUHES4Q&t=1083s))

Now, here are a few quick checks to lock this in. First question, a company wants a vendor's promise of measurable uptime with penalties if the vendor misses it. Which agreement is that, and how does it differ from a memorandum of understanding? A service level agreement, an SLA. It's a binding commitment with measurable targets and consequences. A memorandum of understanding, an MOU, is a looser statement of shared intentions, and it's usually not legally binding. A memorandum of agreement, an an MOA, is firmer. It spells out who will do what. Second question, your company learns that a lawsuit is likely. What must you do right away about your normal data deletion routines?

Suspend them for the relevant data. That's a legal hold. You must preserve everything that could be relevant, even if your retention schedule says it's due for deletion, and you route the details through legal counsel. Third question, a penetration tester spends a week reading the company's job postings and social media before touching any system and is given no inside information. What kind of reconnaissance is that and which environment type? Passive reconnaissance because the tester never touched the target systems in an unknown environment also called black box because no inside information was provided.
