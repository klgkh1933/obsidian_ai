## 00:00 — Protecting data wherever it goes ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7hF2DSks_OU&t=0s))

Picture your company's crown jewels, customer records, product designs, salary spreadsheets. They don't just sit in one place doing nothing. They move around. They get opened and edited. They get copied to laptops and emailed to partners. Protecting data means guarding it wherever it goes, in whatever state it's in, and for as long as it exists. And that's exactly what this lesson is about. In this lesson, you'll learn several key things. First, the kinds and states of data. Second, classification and labeling, from public up to top secret. Third, the eight methods that protect data. Fourth, who is responsible for data, from owners to sub-processors. Fifth, data handling plus data loss prevention, or DLP.

And finally, the data life cycle and the rules that govern different kinds of data. Let's set the stage. You may already know the CIA triad, confidentiality, integrity, and availability. This lesson is where confidentiality gets real. And here's the thing that trips people up. You can't protect all data the same way. A press release needs zero confidentiality. A file full of social security numbers needs a fortress. So, data protection is really three questions asked together. How sensitive is this data? Where is it right now? And who's responsible for it? Answer all three, and the right controls almost pick themselves. Here's a quick tip. When a scenario question asks how to protect some data, look for two clues.

It's classification, meaning how sensitive it is, and its state, meaning at rest, in transit, or in use. The best answer usually matches both, not just one.

## 01:48 — Structured vs unstructured data ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7hF2DSks_OU&t=108s))

Let's start with the two kinds of data. Structured data is organized into a fixed layout of rows, columns, and labeled fields. Think of a spreadsheet or database table. Every customer has a name field, an address field, and a card number field, always in the same place. Because the layout is predictable, structured data is easy to search, easy to encrypt column by column, and easy to mask. Unstructured data has no fixed layout. Emails, documents, chat messages, photos, audio, video, and scanned contracts all fall here. Sensitive information can be hiding anywhere inside them. A social security number pasted into the middle of an email, a customer list buried in a slide deck.

Think of structured data as a filing cabinet with labeled drawers, and unstructured data as the junk drawer in your kitchen. Most organizations hold far more unstructured data, and it's the harder half to protect, because you need tools that scan file contents. Don't assume sensitive data lives only in the database. The same records get exported to spreadsheets and pasted into emails, and those copies are where leaks usually happen. Now, the three states of data.

## 03:01 — Data at rest, in transit and in use ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7hF2DSks_OU&t=181s))

At any moment, data is in one of three states. Think of money. It can sit in a safe, ride in an armored truck, or be spread on a table being counted. Data at rest is stored and not moving. Files, database rows, backups. This is the money in the safe. A stolen laptop hands an attacker the whole vault at once. Data in transit is moving across a network. An email crossing the internet, a file uploading to the cloud. This is the armored truck on the road, and the danger is interception. Data in use is being processed in memory or displayed on a screen. This is the money on the counting table.

It's the trickiest state, because to work with data, a computer usually has to decrypt it first. You can't do math on a locked box. Each state has a go-to protection. For data at rest, you encrypt the storage with full disk, database, or file level encryption. So, a stolen drive is just noise. For data in transit, you encrypt the connection. TLS, which stands for Transport Layer Security, is the secure part of HTTPS. And IPsec, which stands for Internet Protocol Security, is the workhorse of the virtual private network. For data in use, it's the hard one. You rely on strong access controls and clearing memory quickly. And newer approaches use confidential computing, where data stays encrypted even while being processed inside a protected slice of the processor.

If an exam question asks which state is toughest to secure, the answer is in use.

## 04:44 — Data classification and labelling ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7hF2DSks_OU&t=284s))

Now, let's move on to classifying data, because not all secrets are equal. Data classification sorts information into tiers, so you can apply the right level of protection to each one. Think of the clearance stamps on government documents. The objectives name seven classifications, so let's walk through them. Public is meant for anyone. Website copy, published prices, no harm if it leaks. Sensitive is the broad umbrella. It's any data that needs protection because exposure could hurt people or the business. Personal, health, and financial records are the classic examples. Confidential is business information that would cause real damage if leaked. Contracts, forecasts, source code. Restricted is the crown jewels with the tightest access.

Trade secrets, encryption keys, bulk customer records. A leak means serious legal or financial fallout. Critical is a different kind of tier. It's about how badly you need the data to stay available and intact, not only how secret it is. A product price list might be public and still be critical to your sales system. Critical data gets the best backups and redundancy, which you'll cover in the next lesson. Treat these seven as labels, not wrongs on one ladder. A typical company ladder runs public, confidential, restricted. Then there are the government labels. In the United States, confidential is the lowest classified level where a leak could damage national security. Secret is the middle level where a leak could cause serious damage.

And top secret is the highest where a leak could cause exceptionally grave damage. A word of warning here. The word confidential means different things in the two worlds. In a company, it's often a middle to high tier. In a government scheme, it's the lowest classified tier. Read the scenario to see which scheme it's using. Now, labeling is what makes classification usable. Classifying data in a policy document does nothing on its own. Marking and labeling is the act of tagging each file, email, or record with its classification, so both people and machines know how to handle it. A label might be a header on a document, a tag in a file's metadata, a watermark on a printed page, or a banner in an email.

Your automated tools read those labels to decide what to allow. A file labeled restricted can be blocked from leaving the network while a public file sails right through. Here's a quick tip. Classification is deciding the sensitivity level. Labeling is tagging the data with that level. You need both.

## 07:29 — Data roles: owner to subprocessor ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7hF2DSks_OU&t=449s))

The next topic is who's responsible for the data. The objectives name six roles. The data owner is a senior person accountable for a set of data. The owner decides the classification, who may access it, and the handling rules. The data custodian carries out the technical protection day-to-day, storage, backups, encryption, and access controls. Often, that's the IT team. The owner decides, and the custodian enforces. The data steward looks after the quality and proper use of the data, its definitions, its accuracy, and whether people follow the policies around it. The data operator is the person or system that handles the data hands-on during normal work, like the staff member entering orders or the application processing payments.

The data controller is the organization that decides why and how personal data is processed. Under privacy laws like GDPR, the controller carries the main legal responsibility. And the data subprocessor is a third party that a data processor hires to handle personal data on the controller's behalf. The processor handles personal data for the controller. The subprocessor is the processor's own subcontractor. Picture a restaurant. The owner decides who gets the freezer keys. The custodian locks and maintains the freezer. The steward makes sure recipes are accurate, and the operators are the cooks. For the controller chain, imagine hiring a caterer for a wedding. The caterer is the processor. If the caterer hires another firm to deliver the food, that firm is the subprocessor.

You still answer to your guests, so you need to know every link. Here's a quick tip. Match the verb. Owner decides. Custodian safeguards. Steward keeps data accurate. Operator handles it. Controller decides why personal data is used.

## 09:24 — The eight data protection methods ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7hF2DSks_OU&t=564s))

Now, let's talk about the methods used to secure data. There are eight, and encryption is only one. Encryption transforms data so only someone with the right key can read it. It protects data at rest, in transit, and with newer techniques, even in use. Hashing is different. It runs data through a one-way function to make a fixed fingerprint, and you can't turn it back into the original. It proves data hasn't changed and let systems store passwords without keeping the real password. One caution, a social security number has only about a billion possible values. So, an attacker can hash them all and compare. Tokenization replaces sensitive data with a meaningless standing called a token.

The real data gets locked in a separate secure vault. Think of a coat check at a theater. You hand over your expensive coat and get a numbered ticket. If a pickpocket steals the ticket, they get nothing because only the attendant can match it to your coat. A credit card number gets replaced with a random token. Systems can pass that token around, even use it to look up a transaction, but it's useless to a thief. Here's the key difference from encryption. Encryption transforms data mathematically and can be reversed with a key. A token has no mathematical relationship to the original, so there's no math to crack. Data masking hides part of the data while leaving enough visible to be useful.

When a website shows your card as a row of dots ending in 1 2 3 4, that's masking. A call center agent needs the last four digits, not the whole number. Masking can be permanent, like when copying production data into a test system, or dynamic, obscured on screen based on who's looking. Filtering means inspecting data on its way somewhere and blocking or removing the parts that shouldn't go. An email gateway that strips card numbers or a database view that shows only the columns you're allowed to see are filters. De-identification removes or alters the details that tie a record to a specific person, so the data can be used for research, analytics, or testing without exposing who it's about.

Think of a school sharing test scores with a researcher minus the names. There's a catch. If the leftover details are specific enough, a rare job title or a tiny town, someone can match them against other data and work out who's who. That's called re-identification. So, good de-identification also generalizes details like an age range instead of an exact age. Obfuscation is making data or code deliberately hard to read or understand. Masking and tokenization are forms of it. On its own, it isn't real protection. Hiding your house key under the doormat is obfuscation. A deadbolt is encryption. And data transpose, sometimes called data transposition, means rearranging the order of data without changing the values.

You might shuffle the characters inside a field or swap values between rows in a test data set. Picture a shuffled deck of cards. Every card is still there, but you can't rebuild the order. That's also the weakness. If someone spots the pattern, they can undo the shuffle, so it works best alongside stronger tools. To keep the methods straight, ask whether the original can be recovered and by whom. Encryption and tokenization are reversible, but only with the key or vault. Hashing is one-way. Masking and de-identification discard or hide detail.

## 13:12 — Endpoints, geofencing, data location ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7hF2DSks_OU&t=792s))

Now, let's talk about data handling starting with endpoints. Endpoints are the devices people use to work with data, laptops, phones, tablets, and anything that plugs in like USB drives. They're where data gets opened, copied, printed, and lost. So, you use full disk encryption, mobile device management that can lock or wipe a lost phone, limits on removable media, and endpoint data loss prevention. Geofencing draws a virtual boundary around a real-world area and allows or blocks things depending on whether a device or user is inside it. A company might block sign-ins from countries where it has no staff or let a tablet open patient charts only inside the hospital. It's a useful extra layer, but location can be faked, so it never replaces real authentication.

Data location is where the data physically lives. Which country? Which data center? Which cloud region? It matters because location decides which laws apply. And data placement is deciding which kind of data goes into which storage system or network zone. Put your most sensitive data on tightly controlled, encrypted, segmented storage, and not on an internet-facing web server or a shared drive everyone can reach. Location is the where in the world question. Placement is the which system and zone question.

## 14:38 — Data loss prevention (DLP) ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7hF2DSks_OU&t=878s))

The next concept is data loss prevention or DLP. People still email spreadsheets to the wrong address and upload confidential files to personal cloud drives. DLP is the technology that watches for sensitive data trying to leave and stops it. Picture an X-ray scanner in the mail room. It inspects data and matches it against policy using pattern matching for formats like card numbers, keywords, and classification labels, and fingerprinting of known documents. When it spots a violation, it can block, quarantine, alert, or log. In transit, it inspects email and web traffic leaving. At rest, it scans stored files for sensitive data sitting where it shouldn't. And in use, endpoint DLP stops you copying a confidential file to a drive or pasting it into personal webmail.

Tune it too tight and people hunt for workarounds. Too loose and leaks slip through.

## 15:38 — Data life cycle and secure disposal ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7hF2DSks_OU&t=938s))

Now, let's walk through the data management life cycle. There are five stages. Creation is when data is born. Classify and label it now and collect only what you need. Data you never collect can never leak. Management is when data is stored, used, updated, and backed up. Access controls, encryption, and monitoring do their work here. Distribution is when data gets shared inside and outside the company. Encrypted in transit, check its classification before it leaves, and use data loss prevention and contracts to keep recipients in line. Retention is keeping data as long as it's needed and legally required. This is a balancing act. Keep data too long and you're holding a liability, and some laws forbid keeping personal data longer than needed.

Delete too soon and you might violate a requirement to preserve records. A legal hold is a special case. When a lawsuit or investigation is likely, relevant data must be preserved, no matter what the normal schedule says. And disposal is destroying the data securely when retention ends. Deleting a file isn't enough because it usually just removes the pointer while the bits stay on the drive. The reference guide is NIST Special Publication 800-88. Its current version, revision two, came out in September of 2025 and replaced the 2014 version. It sorts sanitization into three levels. Clear uses logical techniques like overwriting. Purge uses stronger techniques that defeat even lab recovery while leaving the device reusable, and destroy makes the media physically unusable.

Your options, from software to sledgehammer, wiping or overwriting replaces the old data with new patterns. Modern drives don't need dozens of passes, and on solid state drives, you should use the drive's built-in secure erase command instead. Cryptographic erasure destroys the encryption key, leaving the data permanently scrambled. It's fast, and sometimes the only option for cloud storage. Degaussing uses a powerful magnetic field on magnetic media. It used to be on the purge list for magnetic drives, but the 2025 NIST revision no longer treats it as an approved method for modern media, and it does nothing useful on solid state drives. And physical destruction means shredding, crushing, or incinerating the drive. Whatever you use, keep a certificate of destruction as your paper trail.

So, the key thing to remember is this: protecting data spans its whole life. Classify it at creation, protect it by state, keep it only as long as law and business need, and sanitize it properly at the end.

## 18:31 — Sovereignty, PCI DSS, GDPR, HIPAA ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7hF2DSks_OU&t=1111s))

Finally, let's talk about location, sovereignty, and the rules others impose. Data sovereignty is a principle that data is subject to the laws of the country where it's physically stored. Store customer data on a server in Germany, and German and European Union law governs it, wherever your company is headquartered. Many privacy laws also follow the person, not just the server. GDPR protects people in the European Union even if the company handling their data sits elsewhere, and it doesn't ban sending data abroad outright. It requires a legal safeguard for the transfer. If a scenario says data must stay in a specific country, any answer that says just move it to the cheapest cloud region is wrong.

The objectives group compliance by kind of data, so match the data in a scenario to the rule. Standards first, PCI DSS, the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard, is required by contract rather than law. Version 4.0.1 is the only active version and every requirement has been mandatory since March of 2025. Never keep the security code or PIN after authorization. Make the card number unreadable wherever it's stored and mask it when displayed. Personal information next, PII, personally identifiable information, is data that can identify a specific person. GDPR, the General Data Protection Regulation, covers personal data about people in the European Union. It gives people rights to see, correct, erase, restrict, move, and object to the use of their data.

It requires most breaches to be reported to the regulator within 72 hours and its top penalties reach 20 million euros or 4% of worldwide yearly turnover, whichever is higher. In the United States, there's no single federal law for general consumer data, but about 20 states, led by California's CCPA, have passed their own. Health data, PHI, protected health information, is health data tied to an individual protected by HIPAA, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. It applies to health care providers, health plans, and their business associates. Affected people must be notified no later than 60 days after a breach is discovered. And it leans on the minimum necessary idea, use only the least information needed.

Financial data, beyond card rules, financial institutions follow GLBA, which requires them to safeguard customer information. And public companies follow SOX, which protects the integrity of financial reporting records. Child and minor data. COPPA, the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, requires verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information from children under 13. The FTC's updated rule has been fully in force since April of 2026, with tighter limits on how long children's data can be kept, and separate consent before sharing it with third parties. Intellectual property is creations of the minds that carry business value, especially trade secrets like formulas, designs, and source code. The law protects a trade secret only if the owner takes reasonable steps to keep it secret.

So, classification, access limits, non-disclosure agreements, and data loss prevention are what keep the legal protection alive. And legal data, like contracts, case files, and communications with lawyers. Attorney-client communications are privileged, so careless sharing can destroy that protection. Records tied to a lawsuit fall under legal hold, and courts can issue legal orders demanding data be produced or preserved. One note on timing. 72 hours is the GDPR breach deadline today. The European Commission has proposed stretching it to 96 hours, but a proposal isn't law, and 72 hours remains the rule. Study the current rules. Let's review what we covered in this lesson. Data is structured or unstructured, and lives in three states.

Encrypt storage at rest, encrypt storage at mine, encrypt connections in transit, and know that in use is hardest. Classification sorts data by sensitivity, and labeling tags each item so people and tools handle it correctly. The owner decides, the custodian protects, the steward keeps data accurate, the operator handles it, the controller decides why personal data is used, and a subprocessor is a processor's subcontractor. Eight methods secure data: encryption, hashing, masking, tokenization, filtering, de-identification, obfuscation, and data transpose. Manage the life cycle from creation through disposal, and location decides which laws apply. PCI DSS covers cards, HIPAA covers health data, GDPR and state laws cover personal data, and COPPA covers children.
