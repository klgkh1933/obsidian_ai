## 01:32 — What an AI model is and does ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a2G9W8QmFCs&t=92s))

## 03:05 — Prompt injection and jailbreaking ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a2G9W8QmFCs&t=185s))

Prompt injection is sneaking malicious instructions into text and AI reads, so it follows them instead of its real instructions. The trouble is that many LLMs mix trusted instructions and untrusted content in the same stream of text and can't reliably tell orders from data. The intern reads every note aloud as if it came from the boss. Direct injection is the user typing the malicious instruction. Indirect injection hides it in content the AI fetches on someone's behalf, like a web page, an email, or a document. In 2025, researchers disclosed a flaw nicknamed Echolak in Microsoft Copilot. A single crafted email, which the victim never had to open or click, could trick the assistant into digging out internal data and sending it to the attacker.

Microsoft fixed it on its end and reported no exploitation in the wild, but the lesson stuck. The AI itself became the attack path. Jailbreaking is persuading a model to break its own safety rules, usually by clever wording, role-play, madeup hypotheticals, stepbystep coaxing. Here's the difference the exam may test. In jailbreaking, the user talks the AI out of its own rules. In prompt injection, a hidden instruction hijacks what the AI is doing, often for a different user.

## 04:30 — Evasion and poisoning attacks ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a2G9W8QmFCs&t=270s))

Evasion is crafting an input that makes a trained model get it wrong at runtime. A few small stickers can make a vision system read a stop sign as a speed limit sign. And attackers tweak malware so a machine learning detector labels it harmless. The model is fine. The input was engineered to fool it. Think of a burglar in a disguise that fools the camera. Family two, corrupting what the model is or what it learns from. Poisoning is corrupting the data a model learns from so it learns the wrong lessons. An attacker might flip labels, slip in bad examples, or plant a hidden trigger so the model misbehaves only when it sees a secret phrase.

Picture sabotaging a spam filters training. So any email with a magic word sails through. Everything else works fine so nobody notices. In 2016, Microsoft launched a chatbot named Tay that learned from conversations on social media. Coordinated users flooded it with abuse and within about 16 hours, Microsoft shut it down. Model manipulation is tampering with the finished model itself, altering its stored parameters, quietly fine-tuning in a back door, swapping a legitimate model file for a doctorred one, or stealing a copy. Here's how to keep the two apart. Poisoning spoils the ingredients. Manipulation tampers with the finished dish. One hedge. The objectives list model manipulation without defining it, and vendors use the phrase as an umbrella for anything that changes how a model behaves.

If an answer list offers it as a general term, think of that umbrella. But if the choices also include poisoning, prompt injection, and evasion, pick the specific one that fits the scenario. Model files come through a supply chain. And in 2024, researchers found around 100 malicious models on a popular public sharing site that ran attacker code the moment a developer loaded them. Here's a quick tip for the lookalikes. Ask when the attack happens. Evasion happens at runtime against a finished model. Poisoning happens at training time.

## 06:42 — Leaks and account takeovers ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a2G9W8QmFCs&t=402s))

Family three, leaks and takeovers. Data loss is sensitive information leaving the organization through AI. The usual culprit isn't a hacker. It's a helpful employee pasting something confidential into a public tool. In 2023, Samsung engineers pasted proprietary source code and meeting notes into chat GPT, and the company then restricted staff use of generative AI. Once data enters a public tool, you may not control where it's stored or whether it feeds future training. Unapproved AI tools are the AI era version of shadow IT. Privacy is the broader risk to people's personal information. Models trained on personal data can memorize and repeat it. Attackers can probe a model to infer whether a particular person's records were used in training.

Chat histories are personal data, too. Privacy laws like the General Data Protection Regulation add obligations around consent and deletion that are hard to honor once data is baked into a model. In plain terms, data loss is your secret leaving through the AI, and privacy is people's personal details being exposed or inferred. Session hijacking means taking over someone's already authenticated session. Stealing a session token, the digital proof you already logged in, lets an attacker walk in without a password or a multiffactor prompt. A hijacked AI session may open a full chat history and every connected tool like email, files, and calendars. In 2026, Octa's researchers reported hundreds of live AI service tokens in a leaked dump of information stealing malware logs and underground marketplaces selling stolen AI login and keys.

Think of a coat check ticket. Whoever holds it gets the coat and nobody asks for identification. Two related twists. ## 15:35 — Indicators of compromise (IOCs) ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a2G9W8QmFCs&t=935s))

OC, which stands for indicator of compromise, is a clue that a breach has happened or is happening right now. It's the muddy footprint that tells you someone's been through the window. The objectives name 12 of them. They fall into four groups. Group one, known bad fingerprints. A hash is a fixed length fingerprint of a file. Defenders keep lists of hashes from known malware, so a match means this exact file is known to be bad. A hash can also reveal tampering if a system files hash changes when it shouldn't. An IP address on a threat list, such as a command and control server that tells malware what to do, is a red flag, and so is traffic to a country you never do business with.

A domain is the same idea by name. Connections to known bad, brand new, randomlooking, or lookalike domains point to trouble, and your DNS logs are where you'll spot them. These three are quick to check against threat feeds, but fragile. Change one bite and the hash is new. Move to a new server and the address and domain are new. That's why behavior-based indicators matter. Group

## 16:47 — IOCs: behaviour on the machine ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a2G9W8QmFCs&t=1007s))

two, behavior on the machine. Malicious processes are unfamiliar programs running, familiar names launched from strange places, or odd pairings like a word processor spawning PowerShell. File system artifacts are the traces left behind. Unfamiliar files in temporary or startup folders, new scheduled tasks, odd registry entries, ransom notes, and files suddenly renamed or encrypted. Timestamps are times that don't add up. Files changed at 3 in the morning, a creation date later than the modified date, logs out of sequence. Attackers sometimes alter timestamps on purpose to blend in, a trick called time stomping. Plain text strings are readable text hiding inside a malicious program or memory dump, like hard-coded web addresses, commands, or passwords, which analysts pull out with strings tools.

The phrase also covers credentials showing up unencrypted in traffic or logs. Say an analyst runs a strings tool on a suspicious file and finds a hard-coded web address in a password. That's plain text strings. And excessive resource consumption is unexplained spikes in processor, memory, disk, or network use. Cryptocurrency mining malware, ransomware encrypting files, a botnet member joining an attack, and data theft all burn resources. In the cloud, it shows up as a surprise bill. ## 18:18 — Lockout, impossible travel, sessions ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a2G9W8QmFCs&t=1098s))

Account lockout is many accounts locking in a short window, which suggests someone is guessing passwords. One lockout is forgetfulness. A pattern is an attack and repeated failures followed by one success may mean the guess worked. But password spraying one or two tries against many accounts often stays under the lockout limit. So there the clue is many accounts each with a failure or two. Impossible travel is a login from New York at 9:00 then Singapore 20 minutes later. No human flies that fast. So either credentials was stolen or a session was hijacked. and concurrent sessions are the same account active in two places at once, especially when policy says one at a time.

It points to stolen credentials, a hijacked session, or password sharing. Group four, the cover up. Log manipulation is logs cleared, edited, shortened, or switched off. Intruders erase their tracks. So, a missing log or a stopped logging service is itself a clue. The fix is to send logs to a separate protected central system so attackers can't scrub them. Here's how the clues read together. An employee scans a quay code on a poster and enters credentials on a fake login page. Minutes later, the account signs in from another continent while the real employee is still logged in. A new scheduled task appears. A strange process starts connecting to a newly registered domain.

Bandwidth spikes and then the audit log is cleared. Each clue alone could be innocent. Together, they tell the story. Here's a quick tip. Think of indicators of compromise as symptoms and the attack type as the disease. Learn to read symptoms backward to the cause. And remember that one clue is weak while a cluster is strong.
