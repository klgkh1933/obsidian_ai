It chains several vectors patiently over months. Message-based vectors are any way an attacker can put words in front of you. Email SMS text messages. RCS, the newer text message standard with read receipts and big media files, instant messaging apps, and collaboration tools like Teams or Slack. The attack is usually fishing. chat feels more personal than email and a message in your work chat looks internal. So this is it. Please approve this login lands with extra weight. Image-based vectors come next because pictures aren't always only pictures.

QR codes, the square barcodes your phone scans, hide the real web address, so you can't judge a link before you open it. Embedded content is hidden stuff inside an image, like an email made entirely of one picture to slit past text filters. And capture, the prove you are human puzzle, shows up as a lure. In the fake capture trick, a page shows a convincing verification box that tells you to press a few keys and paste a command, which secretly installs malware. Real captures never ask you to run commands. Attachmentbased vectors are files that come with a message. Three formats get special attention. Embedded macros are small programs inside office documents.

Microsoft has blocked macros in internet downloaded Office files by default since 2022, but macros that arrive by other roots still bite. RTF documents look like ordinary documents, but can carry hidden objects and have been used to exploit bugs in the programs that open them. and PDF files can contain scripts and links with a fake click here to view the document button as a favorite lure. Browser-based vectors exploit the doorway to everything. Extensions have broad power over the pages you visit. In December 2024, attackers fished a security company's developer account and pushed a malicious update to its extension, exposing data from hundreds of thousands of users. JavaScript runs automatically, so a compromised site can use it to quietly download malware.

Password managers are worth using, but browsers saved passwords are a prime target for credential stealing malware, and a weak master password makes a manager a single point of failure. And cookies and session tokens are the small proofs a site hands your browser after you log in. steal that token and you can walk in without ever knowing the password or passing the multiffactor prompt. Network-based vectors target the plumbing. Infrastructure devices, routers, switches, firewalls, and VPN gateways sit at the edge, face the internet, and often run old software. Vault Typhoon took over small office routers and use them as relay points to hide where its traffic really came from. Virtualized devices, meaning virtual machines and the hypervisor that runs them, can be attacked like physical gear.

And one compromised hypervisor can expose every machine on it. And session keys are the temporary secret keys that scramble one encrypted conversation. Steal them and you can read or hijack that conversation even though the encryption was strong. Remote access is a front door you built on purpose. Remote desktop RDP, usually on port 3389, lets someone control a computer from afar. Exposed to the internet with weak passwords. It's a favorite ransomware door. VNC, a similar screen sharing tool, usually on port 5900, is often set up with a weak or missing password and little encryption. And a VPN is meant to be the safe route in, but stolen login without multiffactor authentication.

And unpatched VPN appliances hand attackers a tunnel straight into the network. Endpointbased vectors target any device a person uses. Workstations, servers, tablets, and mobile devices, which a later lesson on vulnerabilities picks up because that's where users, data, and credentials meet. Trusted devices are machines the network already treats as legitimate like a company managed laptop. Compromise one and the attacker inherits its trust. Then there are the tools already on those devices. Built-in tools are the administration utilities that ship with the operating system like scripting shells. Living off the land tools are legitimate programs that attackers borrow so they never bring their own malware. Antivirus often stays quiet and the only clue is unusual use like an admin tool running at 3:00 in the morning from an accountant's laptop.

## 15:42 — Supply chain vectors ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YHj9rKND0VY&t=942s))

Supply chain vectors go through someone you trust. Why break into a hardened target if you can break into its supplier? That means third-party providers, managed service providers, which are companies that run it for other businesses, logistic providers, the shipping companies that move hardware, where devices can be tampered with in transit, and software as a service providers. Three real cases show the range. In 2020, attackers slit malicious code into a Solar Wind software update, and nearly 18,000 customers received it. In 2021, attackers abused CASSA's remote management software to push ransomware to as many as500 businesses. And in 2025, stolen tokens tied to the SELoft Drift add-on let attackers pull data from Salesforce accounts at more than 700 organizations.

Verizon's 2026 breach report found a third party involved in 48% of breaches. External media is the classic malicious USB drive. Plugging one in can install malware or make the computer think a keyboard is typing commands. Human-based vectors target people. Impersonation means pretending to be the help desk, a delivery driver or the boss. Contractors get access, but often with less vetting and oversight. A visitor can plug in a device, photograph a whiteboard, or tailgate into restricted areas. Biometric attacks fake fingerprints, faces, and voices, and you can't change your fingerprint if it leaks. And a watering hole is when the attacker infects a website your target group is known to visit, then waits like a predator ambushing animals at a watering hole.

IoT vectors are everyday devices with network connections, cameras, sensors, and printers. They often ship with default passwords, rarely get updates, and nobody monitors them. In 2016, the Mai malware hijacked huge numbers of internet connected cameras and recorders by trying factory default login, then used them in a DDoS attack on the DNS provider Dean that made major sites unreachable. OT vectors, short for operational technology, are the equipment that runs physical processes. factory robots, power grid controls, water treatment, pipelines. These systems were built for reliability and safety, not security, run for decades, and can't be patched casually because stopping them stops the plant. In December 2015, attackers took over Ukrainian power distribution systems and cut electricity to roughly 230,000 people for hours.

The lesson is that when IT and OT connect, an IT breach can become a physical event. Physicalbased vectors mean the vector is a door. Lock and key weaknesses include picked or bump locks, copied keys, and keys nobody tracks. An access vestibule, formerly called a man trap, is a small room with two doors where only one can open at a time, so a person can't slip in behind someone else. Its absence is the weakness because without one, tailgating is easy and access passes, badges, and cards can be lost, shared, or cloned. And signal-based vectors ride on radio waves. Bluetooth has suffered from attacks that steal data from discoverable phones or exploit pairing flaws.

RF radio frequency covers everything wireless, including Wi-Fi, wireless keyboards, key fobs, and garage openers. Attackers can jam it, eaves drop on it, or record and replay a fob signal to open a car. And NFC, the tapto pay radio that works within a few centimeters, can be abused by a reader brushed close to your card, or by relay attacks that stretch a legitimate tap across a longer distance. ## 19:52 — Threat intelligence and its life cycle ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YHj9rKND0VY&t=1192s))

Threat intelligence is organized information about current attackers, their methods, and what they're targeting. It's the neighborhood watch bulletin for cyber security. Threat feeds are streams of fresh threat data like lists of known malicious network addresses, web domains or file signatures. You plug them into your defenses so they can block known bad activity automatically. Feeds come from many intelligent sources. Oint open-source intelligence is information gathered from public places like news, social media, public records, and forums. Attackers use it to research you and defenders use it to research you and defenders use it to research them.

Commercial sources are paid feeds and reports from security vendors. Information sharing groups let organizations in the same industry trade intelligence. One of these is called an ISAC, short for information sharing and analysis center, such as the one for financial firms or the one for healthcare. Government sources publish alerts about active threats. Dark web monitoring watches criminal forums for stolen data or chatter about your company. And your own logs tell you what's actually hitting you. One more acronym you'll see throughout domain 2. TPS, which stands for tactics, techniques, and procedures, describes how a threat actor operates. Tactics are the highle goals. Techniques are the general methods. And procedures are the step-by-step actions.

Studying them lets you recognize a group by its behavior, like a burglar's signature style. There's a difference between raw data and true intelligence. A list of a million malicious network addresses is data. Intelligence is knowing which of them are targeting hospitals like yours this week. Data is noise until someone analyzes it and makes it relevant. **Two questions rank threats. Likelihood is how probable it is that a threat will actually be tried against you. Impact is how much damage it would cause if it worked. A nation state targeting a local bakery is low likelihood.** Ransomware hitting it is far more likely. Both likely and damaging goes to the top of the list.

And threat intelligence feeds the likelihood side. Good intelligence is also a loop the intelligence life cycle. Plan and direct deciding what you need to know. Collect the raw data, process it into a usable form, analyze it to find what matters to you, share it with the people who can act and get feedback which reshapes the next round of planning. The objectives list life cycle under threats without defining it. So know both meanings. A threat itself starts when someone discovers a flaw. Then comes disclosure. The riskiest stretch is the gap between disclosure and a patch most people have actually installed when attackers rush to exploit and defenders rush to close it.

Finally, the threat fades as systems get fixed.
