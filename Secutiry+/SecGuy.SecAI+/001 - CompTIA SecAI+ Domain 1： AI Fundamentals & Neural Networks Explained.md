Now let's talk about the engine, the artificial neural network. This architecture is bio inpired. It mimics the human brain's neurons. It works in three stages. Number one, the input layer. This is where the data enters pixels of an image or tokens of text. Number two, the hidden layers. This is the magic black box. In deep learning, we have many of these layers stacked. Each neuron in this layer holds a mathematical value called a weight. Think of a weight like a volume knob. If a piece of data is important, the neuronet turns the weight up.

If it's irrelevant noise, it turns the weight down. Number three, the output layer. This is the final decision. Yes, this is a cat or no, this is not malware. So when we say we are training a model, what are we physically doing? We are just adjusting those millions or billions of weights and biases until the output is correct. That's it. It's not magic. It's calculus. Neural networks have been around for decades. But in 2017, Google released a paper called attention is all you needed. Introducing the transformer architecture. This is what made chat GPT possible. Before transformers, AI read sentences one word at a time, left to right. It often forgot the beginning of the sentence by the time it reached the end.

Transformers introduced a mechanism called self attention. It allows the model to look at the entire sentence at once and weigh the relationship between words simultaneously. It knows that the word bank in bank of the river means land but bank in bank of America means money because of the context provided by the other words. This parallel processing is why modern AI is so fast and surprisingly fluent. Now how do we teach these architectures? There are three main methods you will see on the exam. First, supervised learning. This is learning with a teacher. We give the model labeled data. We show it a picture of a dog and say this is a dog.

We show it malware and say this is malware. The key use case here is spam filters and image classification. The model compares its answer to the ground truth label. Second, unsupervised learning. No teacher, no labels. We throw a massive pile of raw data at the AI and say, "Find the patterns." The AI groups similar items together. This is called clustering. The security use case here is anomaly detection. The AI sees that 99% of your network traffic looks like this. So, the 1% that looks different must be an attack. Third, reinforcement learning or RL. This is training through trial and error. We put an agent in an environment and give it a reward function.

If it does the right thing, like winning a chess game, it gets a point. If it crashes the car, it gets a penalty. It learns to maximize the reward.
