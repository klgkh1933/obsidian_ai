# CompTIA Security+ SY0-801: 2.3 Network, Endpoint, Remote, Supply-Chain, IoT, Physical, Signal Vector

Welcome back to the lab. I am SecGuy. In our last session, we tracked how attackers deceive the human. Today, we track how they exploit the infrastructure. We are closing out objective 2.3 for your Security+ certification. Threat vectors and attack surfaces. We begin at the endpoint. You must defend your workstations, servers, tablets, and mobile devices. But, understand this. Advanced attackers are no longer dropping loud, easily detected malware onto these endpoints. They are using living-off-the-land tools. Living-off-the-land means the attacker uses the operating system's own built-in tools against it. Instead of downloading a custom remote access Trojan, they use native PowerShell, Windows Management Instrumentation, or standard bash scripts to execute their commands. Because these are trusted devices executing cryptographically signed, legitimate binaries, traditional signature-based antivirus completely ignores them.

Look at recent nation-state campaigns like Flax Typhoon. They did not write complex external malware. They hijacked legitimate Windows services and routed their command and control traffic through standard cloud storage to blend in perfectly with normal network traffic. If your endpoint detection relies solely on file hashes, living-off-the-land techniques will bypass you every single time. You must also protect the credentials stored on these endpoints. Password managers are critical for user security, but they introduce a massive single point of failure vector. If an attacker compromises the master vault, they own the entire enterprise. Next, we move to network-based and remote access vectors. Your infrastructure devices, the routers, switches, and load balancers are prime targets.

If an attacker compromises a core router, they can mirror traffic, alter routing tables, and operate invisibly. We also have virtualized devices. If an attacker executes a virtual machine escape and compromises the hypervisor, every single virtual machine running on that host is instantly exposed. But the most heavily abused vectors today are remote access gateways, remote desktop protocols, virtual network computing, or VNC, and legacy virtual private networks, or VPNs. During the shift to remote work, companies open RDP to the public internet. Attackers simply scan the web for port 3389 and brute force their way in. When attackers cannot brute force a password, they steal the active session. This involves session keys, cookies, and session tokens.

When you log into a cloud application, the server issues a session token in your browser cookie, so you do not have to re-authenticate every 5 minutes. If an attacker extracts that cookie from your browser memory using a background information stealer, they can inject it directly into their own browser. They bypass your multi-factor authentication entirely because the cloud server mathematically believes they are already authenticated. To stop this, modern architectures must enforce short-lived session tokens and bind them directly to the device's hardware identity. Now, let us examine the edge, operational technology, Internet of Things, and the physical layer. Internet of Things, or IoT, devices like IP cameras, environmental sensors, and network printers are notorious for shipping with default credentials and unpatchable firmware.

In 2025, the Isuru botnet compromised millions of these devices to launch massive 20 terabit distributed denial-of-service attacks. Operational technology, or OT, controls physical machinery like power grids, manufacturing arms, and water treatment plants. OT prioritizes constant uptime over security. In early 2024, attackers compromised an unsegmented OT network in Muleshoe, Texas, and physically overflowed municipal water tanks. If you do not logically segment your IoT and OT from your corporate network, a compromised lobby printer can lead to a destroyed water pump. Attackers will also bypass your firewalls entirely using physical-based vectors. They will pick a traditional lock and key, tailgate an employee through a secure access vestibule, or physically steal access passes. Once they are physically close to your facility, they leverage signal-based vectors.

They intercept unencrypted Bluetooth connections, capture radio frequency, or RF, transmissions to clone wireless ID badges, and use near field communications, or NFC, to steal access tokens directly from your smartphone while bumping into you in a crowded elevator. Finally, we face the most difficult vector to secure, the supply-chain based vector. Your enterprise relies on third-party providers, managed service providers, logistic providers, and software-as-a-service providers. If a cyber syndicate compromises your managed service provider, they can use the provider's legitimate administrative access to deploy ransomware directly into your network. You did not misconfigure your firewall, but you still got breached because you inherited their vulnerability. We also see external media-based vectors, like a malicious universal serial bus, or USB, drive dropped in a corporate parking lot, waiting for a curious employee to plug it into the network.
