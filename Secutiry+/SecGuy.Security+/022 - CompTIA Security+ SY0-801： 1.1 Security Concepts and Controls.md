## 01:32 — The CIA Triad: Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sEKQuOfkKpk&t=92s))

triad, confidentiality, integrity, and availability. Master this triad, because every framework, policy, and exam question maps directly back to it. First, confidentiality. Confidentiality means keeping secrets private and ensuring that sensitive information is accessible only to authorized individuals. Think about medical records or proprietary source code. If an adversary compromises confidentiality, data has leaked. We protect confidentiality using access control lists, multi-factor authentication, and robust encryption algorithms like AES 256. Next, integrity. Integrity guarantees that data remains accurate, trustworthy, and completely unaltered from its original state. Consider a financial transfer. If an attacker intercepts a transaction and changes the payout from $100 to $10,000, confidentiality was not the primary breakdown. Integrity was. We safeguard integrity using cryptographic hashing algorithms like SHA-256 and digital signatures.

If even a single bit of data is modified in transit or at rest, the hash changes and the tampering is immediately flagged. Finally, availability. Availability ensures that systems, networks, and data are dependable and accessible to authorized personnel whenever they are needed. If your e-commerce platform gets hit with a massive distributed denial-of-service attack on Black Friday or a ransomware strain encrypts your critical databases, availability is destroyed. We engineer availability through high-availability clustering, load balancing, off-site immutable backups, and redundant power systems like uninterruptible power supplies. This brings us to the three-pillar engine of identity, authentication, authorization, and accounting, known as AAA. Think of triple A, like an international flight. First is authentication, proving who you claim to be. At the security checkpoint, you present your government passport. The agent verifies your identity. In technology, we prove identity using multi-factor authentication, combining something you know like a pass phrase, something you have like a hardware security key, or something you are like a biometric fingerprint. Once identity is verified, you reach authorization, determining what you are allowed to touch.

Showing a valid passport does not mean you can walk onto the tarmac or sit in the cockpit. Your boarding pass explicitly dictates your seat and gate. In our architectures, authorization determines whether an authenticated user has read, write, or execute permissions on a resource. The third pillar is accounting, tracking what you did while you were in the environment. Your ticket is scanned at the jet bridge, cameras record your boarding, and your seat assignment is logged. Non-repudiation provides undeniable cryptographic or auditable proof that an action occurred, ensuring that a user or sender cannot falsely deny their actions.

When a software developer signs a production code release using a private cryptographic key, the resulting digital signature guarantees integrity and proves identity. ## 05:53 — Limiting the Blast Radius: Least Privilege & Zero Trust Architecture ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sEKQuOfkKpk&t=353s))

Least privilege dictates that every user, application, and service account must operate with only the minimum permissions necessary to complete their job and strictly for the time needed. If a junior analyst only needs read-only access to customer support tickets, granting them database administrator privileges is an architectural defect. When you combine least privilege with modern environments, you arrive at zero trust. The core mantra of zero trust, codified in NIST special publication 800-207, is simple: never trust, always verify. Zero trust eliminates implicit trust based on network location. Just because a laptop is physically plugged into the office local area network does not make it safe. In a zero trust architecture, every single request, whether from the CEO's corporate workstation or an automated script, must be continuously and explicitly validated.

## 07:11 — Security Controls: The 4 Categories (Technical, Managerial, Operational, Physical) ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sEKQuOfkKpk&t=431s))

we deploy security controls. For your Security+ exam and your day-to-day job, you must understand that every security control is classified along two distinct dimensions: its category, how it is implemented, and its type, the function it performs. Let us start with the four control categories. First, technical controls, also called logical controls. These are hardware and software mechanisms implemented directly within systems to protect resources. Examples include firewall rules, access control lists, endpoint detection agents, and data encryption. Second, managerial controls, sometimes called administrative controls, these are the organizational policies, strategic risk assessments, and governance procedures established by leadership to steer the security posture. Examples include formal acceptable use policies, third-party vendor risk reviews, and data classification frameworks.

Third, operational controls. These are the day-to-day security practices executed by humans to keep operations secure. Examples include conducting security awareness training, running routine vulnerability scans, executing incident response playbooks, and performing manual change approvals. Fourth, physical and environmental controls. These are the tangible real-world measures deployed to protect physical facilities, hardware, and infrastructure. ## 09:01 — Security Controls: The 6 Types (Preventive, Deterring, Detective, Corrective, Compensating, Directive) ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sEKQuOfkKpk&t=541s))

control executes. First, preventive controls. These act as your front-line barricade to proactively block an unauthorized action before it occurs. A locked door, a firewall drop rule, and multi-factor authentication are all preventive. They stop the threat at the point of attempt. Second, deterrent controls. These discourage a threat actor from attempting an attack in the first place by advertising consequences or visible presence. Warning signs that say, "Trespassers will be prosecuted." Visible security guards patrolling a facility and log in banners warning of active monitoring are deterrent controls. They rely on psychological impact. Third, detective controls. These do not stop an attack in progress, but they discover, record, and alert on malicious or unauthorized activity as it happens or shortly after.

Network intrusion detection systems, log auditing, file integrity monitoring, and motion sensors are classic detective controls. They provide essential operational visibility. Fourth, corrective controls. These remediate damage, restore systems, and bring operations back to a secure state after an incident has occurred. Restoring infected service from clean backups, quarantining a malicious host via your endpoint agent, and applying an emergency software patch are corrective actions. Fifth, compensating controls. These are alternative countermeasures deployed when a primary control is technically unfeasible or operationally impractical. If you run a legacy medical device that cannot support modern endpoint agents or patching, you deploy a compensating control. You place that device on an isolated restricted virtual local area network behind a dedicated stateful firewall.

Sixth, directive controls. These mandate specific compliance or behavior through formal rules, policies, and requirements. Standard operating procedures, mandatory compliance training, and clean desk policies serve as directive controls. When evaluating any control on the job or in a scenario, classify both dimensions.
