## 01:16 — Cloud service models and shared responsibility ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mZU9H1nJmJk&t=76s))

and shared responsibility. Cloud services come in three main flavors, defined by how much the provider handles versus how much you do. Infrastructure as a service gives you raw building blocks, virtual machines, storage, and networking. You manage everything on top, from the operating system up. Platform as a service gives you a ready-made platform for your applications. The provider manages the operating system, and you bring your code. Software as a service gives you a finished application, like web-based email. The provider runs nearly everything. A pizza analogy makes this click. Infrastructure as a service is making pizza at home. You get the ingredients, but you do the assembly, the oven, and the cleanup.

Platform as a service is taken bake. It arrives assembled, and you heat it in your own oven. Software as a service is taken bake. It arrives assembled, and you only lift the slice. The more as a service you buy, the less work is yours. That division of labor is the shared responsibility model. The provider secures the underlying infrastructure. That's security of the cloud. You secure what you put into it. That's security in the cloud. Think of renting an apartment. The landlord secures the building. The foundation, the walls, the lobby locks. But you still lock your own front door and decide who gets the key. If you leave your apartment wide open, that's not the landlord's failure.

The exact line moves with the model. With infrastructure as a service, you handle a lot, the operating system, patches, network settings. With software as a service, the provider handles most of it. But one set of duties almost never leaves your hands. A word of warning. In every service model, including software as a service, you still own your data, your user accounts and access decisions, and the security of the devices people use to reach the service. Scenario questions love to make you misread the split. When in doubt, data and access decisions are yours.

## 03:21 — Public, private, hybrid, community cloud ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mZU9H1nJmJk&t=201s))

Now, let's move on to deployment models. Service models tell you what you're renting. Deployment models tell you who else shares the cloud with you. There are four. Public cloud is infrastructure owned by a provider and shared by many unrelated customers, each isolated from the others. It's an apartment building. Lots of tenants, shared structure, private units. Private cloud is cloud style infrastructure used by a single organization. It might sit in your own data center or be hosted for you, but nobody else shares it. It's a house you own. You get more control, and you pay for it in cost and effort. Hybrid cloud combines two or more of these, usually a private environment connected to a public cloud with data moving between them.

It's living in your own house, but renting a storage unit across town. The risk is the link in the middle because every connection is a place where policies can drift apart. And community cloud is shared by several organizations with common concerns, like a group of government agencies or hospitals with the same compliance rules. It's a co-op building where every owner has the same requirements. The members split the cost and the shared compliance burden, but they also share the risk. A related idea is multi-cloud, which means using more than one cloud provider at the same time. Don't confuse it with hybrid. Hybrid mixes private and public. Multi-cloud mixes several public providers.

Companies do it to avoid depending on one vendor, which is called vendor lock-in, to use each provider's best features, and to stay online if one provider has a bad day. The security cost is complexity. Each provider has its own consoles and settings, so your team has to master several, and inconsistent settings between clouds are an easy place for gaps to hide. Here's a quick tip. Public is shared with strangers. Private is yours alone. Community is shared with peers who have the same rules. Hybrid is a mix that talks to each other, and multi-cloud is a separate idea, several providers.

## 05:30 — Serverless, microservices and IaC ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mZU9H1nJmJk&t=330s))

Now, let's talk about cloud-native building blocks. Serverless computing lets you run code without managing any servers. You hand the provider a small function, and it runs on demand, spinning up resources only when something triggers it, and charging only for what runs. The servers still exist. You just never see or patch them. Like taking a taxi instead of owning a car. The provider handles the operating system, which is a real win. But your code, your permissions, and what triggers each function are still yours. A function with far more permission than it needs is a classic least privilege failure, and a poorly protected trigger can let outsiders run your code. Microservices break a big application into many small independent services, each doing one job and talking to the others over the network.

Instead of one giant program, you get a team of specialists. If one fails or needs an update, the others keep running. Like a food court where one stall closing doesn't shut the whole court. From a security view, they're a mixed blessing. You can apply least privilege service by service, and a compromise of one small service doesn't automatically hand over the whole application. But all that network chatter multiplies the connections you have to secure. Each conversation is another door, which is why API security matters so much. Infrastructure as code means defining your servers, networks, and configurations in text files that can be version controlled and deployed automatically instead of clicking through a console.

The benefit is consistency. The same code builds the same environment every time. And you can review changes and rebuild a damaged environment quickly. The catch is that a mistake in the code gets deployed everywhere, instantly and identically. And a password typed into a template can leak to anyone who can read the code. Here's a quick tip. Infrastructure as code is a double-edged sword on the exam. Review it like any other code, and never hardcode secrets in it.

## 07:31 — On-premises, air-gapped and OT ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mZU9H1nJmJk&t=451s))

The next concept is the models beyond the cloud. On-premises means you own and run the servers, storage, and network and facilities you control. That control is the appeal. The trade-off is that you also own everything that goes wrong. Hardware, power and cooling, physical security, patching and disaster recovery. It's owning the house. Nobody else can raise the rent and nobody else will fix the roof. An air gap network is physically isolated from other networks including the internet. There's literally a gap of air. No cable, no wireless link. Air gaps protect the most sensitive systems like classified government networks and nuclear plant controls, but it isn't magic. Air gap systems are also harder to patch and monitor.

Data still has to get in and out somehow. Usually on removable media like USB drives and that's the weak spot. The famous Stuxnet attack reportedly crossed an air gap on an infected drive. A heads-up. Air gap means no network connection, not unhackable. When a question describes an air gap system being compromised, look for removable media or a careless insider. OT or operational technology is the hardware and software that controls the physical world. Factory robots, power grid controls, water treatment, building heating and cooling. You'll hear names like ICS, industrial control systems, SCADA and PLCs, the small rugged computers that run the machines. Ordinary IT moves data. OT moves things and a failure can cause physical damage or endanger lives.

That changes how you secure it. Safety and availability come first. A power plant can't reboot for a patch on Tuesday afternoon. Equipment lives for decades, so control is installed years ago may run obsolete systems that can't be patched. Many devices have very little computing power so they can't run security software or heavy encryption, and they often speak proprietary protocols. So, you protect OT mainly by surrounding it. Segment it away from the corporate network, tightly control every connection in, and in the most critical cases, air gap it.

## 09:52 — Logical vs physical segmentation ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mZU9H1nJmJk&t=592s))

That brings us to segmentation. Logical segmentation separates systems with software settings on shared hardware. VLANs, subnets, firewall rules, and the virtual networks and security groups you configure in the cloud. It's flexible, cheap, and quick to change, but a misconfiguration can let traffic cross a boundary that should have held. Physical segmentation uses separate hardware. Different switches, different cables, even different buildings. Nothing is shared, so there's nothing to misconfigure into a leak. The cost is money, space, and flexibility. Logical segmentation is a curtain dividing an office. Physical segmentation is two separate buildings. Most designs use both. Logical for everyday separation, and physical for the crown jewels.

## 10:39 — Technical factors in architecture choice ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mZU9H1nJmJk&t=639s))

Now, let's cover the technical considerations that drive an architecture choice. Choosing an architecture is a trade-off exercise. Availability asks how much uptime you need. Cloud providers offer strong uptime commitments, and on-premises system depends on your own redundancy. Resilience is different. Availability is how often you're up. Resilience is how well you absorb a failure or attack and bounce back. Proprietary versus open source is a classic. Proprietary software is closed. You get vendor support, but you can't inspect the code. You wait on the vendor's patches, and you risk lock-in. Open source code is public. Anyone can inspect it, and you're not tied to one vendor. But, nobody's obliged to support it. You're responsible for patching it, and many eyes don't guarantee someone actually looked.

The Heartbleed flaw sat in widely used open source for years. And in 2024, a hidden backdoor was found in a popular open source compression library. Neither is automatically safer. Usability matters because security that frustrates people gets bypassed, and staff will find a faster, riskier path. Responsibility asks who patches, monitors, and responds, and gaps appear where each side assumes the other has it covered. Compute asks whether the platform has the processing power. Cloud scales on demand, while small OT devices have very little. Power requirements remind you servers need steady electricity and cooling, which you provide on premises, and the provider handles in the cloud. And ease of recovery asks how fast you can get back.

Cloud snapshots and infrastructure as code can rebuild an environment in minutes. An aging on-premises server or OT controller takes days.

## 12:32 — Business factors and data sovereignty ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mZU9H1nJmJk&t=752s))

Now, the business considerations. Data sovereignty means data is subject to the laws of the country where it's stored. Choosing a cloud region also chooses which laws apply. Data classification says the more sensitive the data, the more control you need over where it lives. Highly classified data may demand a private environment or an air gap. Cost matters because cloud usually shifts money from CapEx, large upfront purchases, to OpEx, ongoing pay-as-you-go fees, while on premises is the opposite. Ownership asks who owns the hardware, the software, and the data, and how you get your data back if you leave. Environmental requirements cover power, cooling, humidity, fire suppression, and flood protection, which you provide on premises, and the provider handles in the cloud.

Scalability asks whether the system can grow or shrink when demand changes. Cloud does it quickly. On premises means buying hardware. And risk means every option carries some, and the right architecture is the one whose risks the business is willing to accept. Here's the key takeaway. There's no best architecture, only the best fit. Exam scenarios hand you a need, strictest isolation, fastest growth, lowest upfront cost, a regulated data set, and ask you to match it. Read for the deciding factor, then pick the model that serves it.

## 14:00 — Zero trust and APIs in the cloud ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mZU9H1nJmJk&t=840s))

Now for zero trust in the cloud. You met the core of zero trust earlier. Never trust, always verify, and treat identity as the new perimeter. That fits the cloud naturally, because there's no perimeter to defend. The cloud delivered tools that enforce it, the security service edge services were covered in the network design lesson. What matters here is that with no network wall to hide behind, identity becomes the perimeter. Every request from any user or service must prove who it is and be authorized for exactly what it's asking, every time. That makes your identity provider, the service that holds your accounts and vouchers for them, one of your most valuable targets.

That extends to machines talking to machines. An API, or application programming interface, is the defined way one piece of software where requests something from another. Cloud applications are stitched together with APIs, and every API is a door. AP security means authenticating every caller, authorizing each request, validating input, and limiting how fast anyone can call.

## 15:12 — Cloud misconfiguration breaches ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mZU9H1nJmJk&t=912s))

Now for a fact that surprises newcomers. In the cloud, many breaches don't come from clever hackers breaking encryption. They come from customers accidentally leaving things open. Misconfiguration is one of the most common causes of cloud data exposure and one of the most preventable. Stolen credentials are the other big cause. The usual suspects, publicly exposed storage. A cloud storage bucket set to public spills its contents to anyone who finds the address. That's the classic headline breach. Overly permissive access which violates least privilege. Hard-coded or default credentials where a key typed into code ends up in a public repository and unlocks a whole account. Disabled logging which removes the records you'd need to investigate.

And unrestricted network rules left open to the entire internet. The frustrating part is that these are all your responsibility, not the provider's. The building was secure. Someone left an apartment door open. Here's a quick tip. When a cloud scenario describes exposed data with no sign of an attacker breaking anything, suspect misconfiguration first. The best answer is usually tightening a setting, not buying a new product. Why is it so common? Cloud platforms offer thousands of settings. Defaults sometimes favor convenience and infrastructure as code can copy one bad setting across hundreds of resources in seconds. So the defense is layered. CSPM, cloud security posture management, is software that continuously scans your cloud accounts against best practices and flags risky settings, a public bucket, an open network rule, a disabled log.

It's a building inspector who walks the whole complex every night and hands you a list of unlocked doors. That's a detective control. Pair it with guardrail policies that block dangerous settings before deployment, a preventive control, and with infrastructure as code that's reviewed like any other code.

## 17:14 — Two architecture scenarios ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mZU9H1nJmJk&t=1034s))

Let's apply this in two quick scenarios. First, a company stores customer files in a cloud bucket and the researcher reports anyone on the internet can download them. Work the shared responsibility model. Storage access settings are the customer's job on every model, so this isn't the provider's failure. The cause is almost certainly a bucket set to public. The best immediate fix is to make it private and restrict access, then review the logs to see who reached the data. Going forward, posture management would flag the setting, least privilege would limit the damage, and logging lets you investigate. Second, a regional hospital group must keep patient records inside its country, wants a shared tightly regulated platform with partner hospitals, and needs a public appointment website that handles sudden spikes.

The patient records are sensitive and bound by data sovereignty, so they belong in a private or community environment in a region inside the country. The website holds no sensitive data and needs elastic capacity, so it fits the public cloud. Connecting the two makes the design hybrid, and that connection needs encryption and strict access rules. Read for the deciding factor, and the right model follows. Let's review what we covered in this lesson. Cloud comes as infrastructure, platform, or software as a service. Make it, bake it, or have it delivered. The provider secures the cloud and you secure what's in it, always keeping your data and your access. Deployment models say who shares the building.

Public, private, community, and hybrid. Multi-cloud means several providers with flexibility and complexity. Serverless, microservices, and infrastructure as code are the cloud-native building blocks. On-premises gives you full control and full responsibility. Air gapped networks isolate sensitive systems but fall to removable media. OT runs physical processes, favors safety, and is protected by segmentation. Logical segmentation uses settings. Physical segmentation uses separate hardware. Weigh technical factors like availability, resilience, and recovery, and business factors like sovereignty, cost, and risk to find the best fit. And in the cloud, identity is the perimeter, and misconfiguration is a major preventable cause of breaches.

## 19:49 — Quick self-check questions ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mZU9H1nJmJk&t=1189s))

Now, here's a quick check to lock it in. First question. In a software as a service model, who is responsible for controlling who can access your data? You are, the customer. In every service model, data and access decisions stay with you. The provider secures the platform, not your choices about your data. Second question. A hospital must keep records inside its country and also host a public website that handles traffic spikes. Which deployment models fit and why? Keep the records in a private or community cloud in a region inside the country because of data sovereignty and sensitivity. Put the public website in the public cloud for elastic capacity. Connecting them makes it a hybrid design.

Third question. Someone says an air gapped network can't be compromised. Why they're wrong? Because an air gap only removes the network connection. Data still crosses on removable media like USB drives or through insiders, and that's how attacks like Stuxnet reportedly crossed the gap. The best defense is controlling and scanning removable media. Next, you'll follow the data itself, learning to protect it at rest, in transit, and in use.
