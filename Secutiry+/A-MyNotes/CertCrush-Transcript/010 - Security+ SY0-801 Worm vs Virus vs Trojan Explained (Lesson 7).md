## 01:22 — The malware zoo: virus and worm ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRbUTF6LXm4&t=82s))

Let's start with the malware zoo. Malware is any software written to cause harm. It's short for malicious software. The exam doesn't only want the word, it wants you to tell the species apart by how each one behaves. We'll begin with the spreaders, viruses and worms. A virus is malicious code that attaches to a legitimate file or program and needs a person to run it before it spreads. Like a biological virus, it needs a host. Open the infected file and it activates and tries to infect more files. A worm is self-spreading malware that needs no host and no human action. It crawls across networks on its own, exploiting weaknesses to jump from machine to machine.

Where a virus waits to be invited, a worm lets itself in. That difference is a classic exam question. Here's a quick tip. A worm equals self-spreading, no host, no user needed. A virus equals needs a host file and a user action to run. If a scenario says the infection spread across the network overnight with nobody clicking anything, that's a worm. Next, the disguise, Trojans.

## 02:33 — Trojans: the disguise ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRbUTF6LXm4&t=153s))

A Trojan, short for Trojan horse, is malware disguised as something you want. It looks like a free game, a useful utility, or a cracked app, but hidden inside is a payload that attacks once you run it. The Greeks hid soldiers inside a giant wooden horse that the Trojans wheeled through their own gates. The gift was the attack. A Trojan doesn't self-replicate. It relies entirely on tricking you into installing it, which is why Trojans and social engineering go hand-in-hand. A famous example is Emotet, which started in 2014 as a banking Trojan, grew into a delivery service for other criminals' malware, and was knocked offline in January of 2021 by a multi-country police operation.

## 03:22 — Ransomware: the hostage-taker ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRbUTF6LXm4&t=202s))

Now, the hostage taker, ransomware. Ransomware encrypts your files and demands payment for the key to unlock them. It's a kidnapping, except your files are the hostage. Modern ransomware often adds a second squeeze called double extortion. The attackers copy your data before encrypting it, then threaten to leak it publicly unless you pay, even if you have backups. So, good backups protect availability, but not confidentiality against this trick. Real attacks blur the categories. In May of 2017, WannaCry hit more than 200,000 computers in over 150 countries. It carried a ransomware payload, but spread like a worm, jumping between unpatched Windows machines with nobody clicking anything. Hospitals in the United Kingdom's National Health Service were among the victims.

If a scenario describes both behaviors, name both. A word of warning. Paying the ransom is never guaranteed to work, and it funds the next attack. The real defense is solid, tested, offline, or immutable backups, which a later lesson covers. An immutable backup can't be encrypted or deleted by the attacker, so you can restore instead of pay.

## 04:36 — Spyware, keyloggers and adware ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRbUTF6LXm4&t=276s))

Now, the watchers, spyware, keyloggers, and adware. Spyware secretly watches you and collects information, meaning browsing habits, credentials, screenshots, and files. Symptoms often include a sluggish system, unexpected pop-ups, and data leaving the network that shouldn't be. A keylogger is a specific kind of spyware that records every key you press, capturing passwords as you type them. Software keyloggers hide on the computer. Hardware keyloggers are tiny devices plugged in between the keyboard and the computer, so an attacker with a few seconds of physical access can install one. Adware pushes unwanted advertising at you, pop-ups, banners, browser redirects, a homepage you never chose. It usually arrives bundled with free software. At its mildest, it's an annoyance.

At its worst, it quietly tracks what you browse and starts to look a lot like spyware. The exam clue is advertising behavior you didn't ask for.

## 05:38 — Rootkits and fileless malware ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRbUTF6LXm4&t=338s))

Now, the hiders, rootkits, and fileless malware. A rootkit buries itself deep in the operating system, sometimes down in the firmware, to hide its presence and give the attacker persistent privileged access. Rootkits are hard to detect precisely because their job is to stay invisible. They can even lie to your antivirus. When a scenario says the scanner reports clean, but the system is plainly compromised, think rootkit. The usual cure is drastic, rebuild the machine from a known good image. In 2005, a researcher discovered that copy protection software on millions of Sony Music discs installed on listeners' computers and hid itself using rootkit techniques. Nobody had agreed to it. It was hard to remove and other malware soon took advantage of the hiding place it created.

Hiding is the rootkit's signature, whoever wrote it. Fileless malware runs in memory using legitimate tools already on the system, like PowerShell, instead of writing a malicious file to disk. Because there's no file to scan, traditional antivirus often misses it. It often starts when someone opens a malicious document and a macro launches a script. Defenders catch it by watching behavior, not files, which is what EDR, endpoint detection and response, is built for. Using a system's own tools against it is called living off the land, which you met earlier as a threat vector.

## 07:07 — Logic bombs, botnets, backdoors ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRbUTF6LXm4&t=427s))

Next, the patient one, the logic bomb. A logic bomb is dormant code set to trigger on a condition, like a specific date, or an employee's account being disabled. It sits quietly until the trigger fires. Logic bombs are the classic insider weapon. In 1996, a network administrator at the manufacturer Omega Engineering was fired. Weeks later, the company's production programs were wiped by a time-triggered program he had planted. The damage topped $10 million and he was convicted. The attacker was long gone and the bomb did the work on a schedule. So, the tell is timing. Damage that begins on an exact date or right after a specific event like a termination points to a logic bomb.

A few supporting characters show up in scenarios, though they're not on the objective list. A botnet is a network of infected machines, each one called a bot or a zombie, controlled remotely by an attacker. In 2016, the Mirai botnet, built mostly from hacked cameras and home routers, knocked a major DNS provider offline and took a long list of popular websites down with it. A backdoor is a hidden way back into a system that bypasses normal login. A remote access Trojan is a Trojan whose whole purpose is to give the attacker remote control, and a potentially unwanted program is software that isn't outright malicious, but drags in extras like bundled toolbars.

Several of these work together. A Trojan sneaks in, installs a rootkit to hide, opens a backdoor, and quietly adds your machine to a botnet. One infection, many jobs. When a scenario describes a chain like that, identify each piece by the role it plays. Here's an analogy that ties them together. Think of your computer as a house. Spyware is a hidden camera. A rootkit is a squatter who's rewired the house so you can't tell they live there. A logic bomb is a device rigged to go off at midnight. And fileless malware is an intruder who uses your own ladder and your own keys, so they never leave fingerprints. Try a few.

Overnight, 300 workstations show a ransom note, and nobody opened anything. That's ransomware with a worm spreading behavior like WannaCry. A user installs a free video converter, and a week later their banking passwords are in use elsewhere. That's It's probably carrying a key logger or spyware. Antivirus finds no files, but monitoring shows PowerShell on an accounting laptop making odd outbound connections. Fileless malware. The key thing to remember is this, learn malware by its behavior and its clue. Spreads alone is a worm, needs a click is a virus, disguised is a Trojan, encrypts and demands payment is ransomware, hidden and persistent is a rootkit, fires on a date or event is a logic bomb.

No file on disk is fileless.

## 10:09 — Physical attacks and defences ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRbUTF6LXm4&t=609s))

Now, let's move on to physical attacks. Not every attack arrives over a wire. Some walk through the front door. Four are named in the objectives. Tailgating is following an authorized person through a secure door before it closes without their approval. The attacker carries a coffee in each hand or a big box or wears a delivery uniform so a polite stranger holds the door. You'll also hear piggybacking and most sources treat the two as near synonyms. Some instructors draw a line with tailgating meaning slipping in behind someone who doesn't notice and piggybacking meaning going in with someone who knowingly lets you through, but that split is a teaching convention, not an official one.

Read the scenario for the clue and don't sweat the label. The best fix is an access vestibule, a small two-door entry space where only one person passes at a time, also called a mantrap. Add badge per person turnstiles, guards, and a culture where asking, "Can I see your badge?" is normal. Shoulder surfing is watching someone enter a password, a PIN, or sensitive data by looking over their shoulder or from across a cafe with a camera. Privacy screens, covering the keypad, and multi-factor authentication are the standard defenses. Skimming is capturing payment card data with a hidden device. The classic version is a fake card reader fitted over a real one on an ATM, gas pump, or checkout terminal, often with a tiny camera to grab the PIN.

A related trick pulls data from contactless cards with a handheld reader. Defenses include inspecting readers for anything loose or bulky, tamper evidence seals, and using chip and mobile wallet payments instead of swiping. Forced entry is the loud one. Kicking a door, prying a frame, cutting a lock, smashing a window. The attacker accepts the noise because the payoff is worth it. Strong doors and frames, quality locks, alarms, cameras, and good lighting raise the cost. Forced entry is hard to stop, but easy to detect, which is why alarms and cameras matter. Here's a quick tip. Match the fix to the trick. Tailgating points to vestibules and badge checks. Shoulder surfing points to privacy screens.

Skimming points to tamper checks and chip payments. Forced entry points to hardened doors, plus alarms and cameras. One more classic, though it's not on the list, is dumpster diving. Digging through trash for discarded documents or old hard drives. Shredders are the answer.

## 12:48 — Social engineering: hacking people ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRbUTF6LXm4&t=768s))

Now, let's move on to social engineering, which is hacking the human. The strongest firewall in the world doesn't help if an attacker convinces your employee to hand over the password. Social engineering is manipulating people into breaking security or giving up information. It attacks the human, not the machine, and it's behind a huge share of real breaches.

## 13:10 — The phishing family and its variants ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRbUTF6LXm4&t=790s))

Let's start with a phishing family. Phishing is a fraudulent message that tries to trick you into clicking a malicious link, opening an attachment, or revealing credentials. It's the broadest and most common social engineering attack. Picture different fishing gear. Phishing is casting a wide net, generic messages blasted to thousands hoping a few bite. Spear fishing is aiming a spear at one specific person or organization using details about them to seem legitimate. And whaling is harpooning the whale, going after a high value target like a chief executive or chief financial officer where one catch is worth a fortune. The delivery channel names the rest. Vishing is voice fishing over a phone call.

Smishing is SFMS fishing over text messages and quishing is fishing through a QR code. A malicious web address is hidden inside the code which might sit in an email, on a poster, or on a sticker pasted over a legitimate code on a parking meter. It works because email filters that scan text links often can't read a link buried in an image and because the victim scans with a personal phone that sits outside the company security tools. Here's a quick tip. The delivery vector names the attack. Voice call, vishing. Text message, smishing. QR code, quishing. Email, phishing. Same trick, different channel. Three more ideas. Impersonation means pretending to be someone you're not like IT support, a delivery driver, an auditor, or your boss in person, by phone, by email, or with a fake voice and face.

Pretexting is the believable backstory that makes the impersonation work. Hi, this is Dana from the help desk. We're doing a password audit. And BEC, business email compromise, is a targeted scam where the attacker impersonates an executive or trusted vendor, usually to trick someone in finance into wiring money or changing payment details. Many of these emails contain no malware and no suspicious link, so technical filters have nothing to catch. The defense is process. A callback on a known number and two people approving any payment change. So, why does social engineering work? Attackers pull specific psychological levers. Urgency, act now or your account

## 15:31 — Urgency, authority and trust ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRbUTF6LXm4&t=931s))

will be closed. Authority, pretending to be the boss, the police, or IT. Scarcity, only three spots left. Fear, you'll be fined unless you pay immediately. Familiarity and trust, posing as a colleague or a brand you like. And social proof, everyone on your team already did this. Here's a worked example. An employee gets a text, "This is Dana from IT. We've detected a breach on your account. Reply with your login code in the next 5 minutes or we'll lock you out." Name the levers. It's a text, so it's smishing. It claims to be IT, that's authority and impersonation. The 5-minute deadline is urgency and the breach is fear. Four levers in one message.

That stacking is a hallmark of a real attempt. Every lever bypasses careful thinking. That's the point. The universal defense is the same across every variant. When a message pressures you to act fast, stop and verify through a separate trusted channel before you do anything.

## 16:39 — Deepfakes and impersonation ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRbUTF6LXm4&t=999s))

Now, deepfakes. A deepfake is AI-generated fake audio, video, or imagery that convincingly imitates a real person. A cloned voice on the phone. A video call with a face that looks exactly like your chief executive's. It's impersonation with a counterfeit face and voice and it has already cost real companies real money. In 2019, the chief executive of a United Kingdom energy company got a call that sounded exactly like his German parent company's boss, accent and rhythm included. He wired about 220,000 euros to a supplier within the hour. In 2024, a finance employee at the engineering firm Arup in Hong Kong joined a video call with what looked like the company's chief financial officer and several colleagues.

Everyone else on the call was an AI fake. The employee made 15 transfers worth roughly 200 million Hong Kong dollars, about 25 million United States dollars. And in 2025, the FBI warned that attackers were using AI-generated voice messages and texts to impersonate senior government officials. A deep fake is a counterfeit of a person. Like a forged signature copies your handwriting, a deep fake copies your face and voice. And just as you verify a suspicious check by calling the bank, you verify a suspicious boss by calling them back on a number you already know. A word of warning. Don't build a defense on I'd spot a fake. People can't reliably tell a good clone from the real thing.

The defenses that work are process defenses. Call backs to a known number, a pre-agreed code word, two approvers for large payments, and a culture where questioning an ex-executive is praised. Deep fakes can also fool voice and face authentication, so be skeptical of any system relying on either alone.

## 18:37 — Review and key takeaways ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRbUTF6LXm4&t=1117s))

Let's review what we covered in this lesson. Malware sorts by behavior. Viruses need a host and a user. Worms self-spread. Trojans disguise themselves. Ransomware holds files hostage. And spyware, adware, keyloggers, rootkits, logic bombs, and fileless malware each have a signature trick. Physical attacks each have a matching control. Vestibules and badge checks for tailgating, privacy screens for shoulder surfing, tamper inspection, and chip payments for skimming, and hardened doors with alarms and cameras for forced entry. Social engineering attacks people, not machines. Phishing, spear phishing, whaling, vishing, smishing, and quishing differ by target and channel, while impersonation, pretexting, and deep fakes supply the believable story. The universal defense is to verify urgent requests through a separate trusted channel, and deep fakes make process defenses like callbacks and two approvers matter more than ever.

## 19:40 — Quick self-check questions ([↗](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DRbUTF6LXm4&t=1180s))

Now, let's do a few quick checks. Here's the first one. A worm and a virus both spread, but differently. What's the key distinction? A worm is self-spreading. It needs no host file and no human action, crawling across the network on its own. A virus needs a host file to attach to and needs a person to run it before it spreads. If nobody clicks anything and it spread overnight, that's a worm. Here's the second check. An employee gets an urgent text claiming to be from IT demanding their login code within 5 minutes or their account gets locked. What kind of attack is this and which psychological levers are being pulled? This is smishing because it arrives by text message.

The levers are authority since it claims to be IT, urgency from the 5-minute deadline, and fear from the threat of being locked out. And here's the third. A finance employee joins a video call with what looks like the chief financial officer who urgently asks for a wire transfer. What's the likely attack and what's the best defense? It's a deep fake used for impersonation. The best defense is a process, not a sharp eye. Verify by calling the executive back on a known number and require a second approver for large payments.
