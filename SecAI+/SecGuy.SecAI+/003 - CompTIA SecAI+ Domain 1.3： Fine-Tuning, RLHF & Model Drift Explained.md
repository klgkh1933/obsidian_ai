These are the concepts that define how safe or unsafe a model actually is. Let's start with transfer learning. Think of a pre-trained model like GPT4 as a college graduate. It has general knowledge about history, math, and coding.

It's smart, but it doesn't know your company. It doesn't know your network topology or your internal policies. Fine-tuning is the process of taking that general graduate and giving them on the-job training. We take a pre-existing model and train it further on a smaller specific data set. This is efficient, but it introduces supply chain risk. If the base model you downloaded from HuggingFace has a hidden back door, your fine-tuned model has it, too. We'll talk about how to detect that in domain 2. Now, how do we stop this model from being toxic? How do we stop it from telling users how to build a bomb? We use RLHF, reinforcement learning, from human feedback.

This is a specific type of training where humans rank the AI's responses. The model learns, "Oh, humans like it when I'm helpful and safe, and they hate it when I'm racist or dangerous." This feedback loop creates the guard rails of the model. When you see a jailbreak attack, what the attacker is really doing is bypassing the RLHF conditioning. But how do we know if the model is actually working? You need to know the confusion matrix for the exam. This is the scorecard. If an AI correctly spots malware, that is a true positive. If it misses the malware, that is a false negative. That is the most dangerous error in security.

If it flags a safe file as malware, that is a false positive. Too many of these cause alert fatigue. You need to balance precision being right with recall, finding everything. Even if you get the metrics right today, they won't stay right forever. This is called drift. Data drift happens when the real world changes, like hackers inventing a new language that your model wasn't trained on. Concept drift is when the definition of safe changes. If you don't monitor for drift, your fancy AI security tool becomes obsolete in 6 months. Finally, we have to talk about the biggest risk in domain one, hallucination. Remember, LLMs are probabilistic. They don't know facts. They only predict the next likely word.

A hallucination is when an AI gives you a confident, coherent answer that is completely factually wrong. This brings us to a critical exam concept, temperature. This is a hyperparameter, a setting that controls the model's creativity. A high temperature like 0.8 makes the model creative and random. A low temperature like 0.2 makes it precise and boring. If you need to stop hallucinations in a security tool, the answer is almost always lower the temperature. This is compounded by the blackbox problem. Because these models use billions of parameters, we often cannot explain why they chose that specific lie. For compliance, like GDPR or the EU AI act, this is a nightmare.
